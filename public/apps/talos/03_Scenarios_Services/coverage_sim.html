Orbit & coverage simulator

Fly any TALOS configuration — one satellite, a formation cluster or a Walker constellation — over a day or two and see where it looks, how often it comes back and what a chosen point of interest gets in passes, minutes and gaps. Everything is computed in the browser from circular J2 orbits; the defaults are the TALOS-1 baseline (—) over Athens.

How to use the controls

Altitude (400–700 km)
Sets the orbital period (about 92 to 99 minutes), the footprint radius (at 5° elevation about 1,800 km at 400 km and 2,300 km at 700 km) and the slant range that drives the link budget on the emitters page. Higher is more coverage per pass but weaker signals and more disposal ΔV; the baseline is 530 km.
Inclination (30–98°)
The highest latitude the ground track reaches. 53° is the baseline because it maximises passes over 30–45°N; the "SSO" button sets the sun-synchronous value computed from J2 (97.5° at 530 km), the orbit that rideshare launches offer most often and the one that gives a polar ground station a contact every orbit.
Epoch
Start time of the simulation; it fixes where the planes sit relative to the Earth. Changing it shifts the pass times but not the statistics.
Constellation type
Single is TALOS-1. Formation cluster places N satellites on the same track separated by the along-track spacing, with alternate satellites offset cross-track by the chosen amount through a small RAAN difference (the TALOS baseline is 150 km and ±20 km). Walker is the classic delta pattern: P planes evenly spaced in RAAN, S satellites per plane, phasing F between planes. Walker of clusters puts a three-satellite formation in every Walker slot — this is how the geolocation-capable stages 3 and 4 are actually built. Custom accepts any list.
Minimum elevation (5–30°)
The elevation mask at the ground point. 5° counts everything the satellite can physically see; 10–20° is closer to the "useful RF geometry" within about 1,000 km ground range where geolocation is good; 30° is a strict nadir-ish cone. Pass counts fall quickly as the mask rises.
Duration (6–48 h)
Statistics are normalised per day. A single inclined satellite needs at least 24 h to show its two groups of passes; 48 h smooths the figures for sparse constellations. The ground-track precession of the 53° orbit (about 5°/day of RAAN drift) does not matter on this scale.
Grid resolution
Cell size and time step of the coverage grid. Medium (4°, 60 s) with 12 satellites over 24 h computes in well under a second; fine (2.5°, 45 s) is a few seconds for large constellations. The status badge on the map shows "computing…" while the grid runs; the rest of the page stays live. If the estimated work is excessive the simulator coarsens the grid and says so.
Heat layer metric
Mean revisit gap is the average time between successive accesses of a cell; maximum gap is the worst wait; accesses per day counts separate visibility windows. Cells never seen are left blank. The legend adapts its scale to the 90th percentile of the data so that a single satellite and a 24-satellite constellation both show structure.
Point of interest
Eight presets cover the scenarios used elsewhere in the study (Aegean, Cyprus, Black Sea, Suez, Libya, Hormuz, Taiwan Strait, Arctic); type coordinates or click the map. The KPIs use exact pass computation at 20 s steps, not the grid, so they are more precise than the heat layer.
Animation
Play advances simulation time by the chosen number of seconds per frame and moves satellites, footprints and the track windows; drag the time slider to any instant. The grid and KPIs cover the whole duration and do not change with the slider.

Experiments worth running

  • Baseline vs SSO for Athens. Keep one satellite, press "SSO", and compare passes per day and maximum gap. The research note's estimate that SSO costs roughly 40 % of mid-latitude revisit should appear directly[R4-17]; then move the point to Svalbard and watch the ranking invert.
  • Mask sensitivity. Raise the minimum elevation from 5° to 20° with one satellite: the pass count drops from about seven to the "2–4 useful passes" quoted in research note 04 §6.1, and the mean pass length falls from eight minutes to four.
  • Cluster vs singles. Switch between a Walker of 4 × 3 singles and a Walker of 4 clusters: identical satellite counts, three times the revisit nodes, and a maximum gap over Athens that differs by a factor of about two. The single vs constellation page discusses why the cluster is nevertheless the right first step.
  • HawkEye-class persistence. Eight planes × 2 clusters (48 satellites) at 53° give sub-20-minute mean gaps at 38°N; HawkEye's "under 45 minutes globally" with 13–15 clusters mixes SSO and 40–46° planes to get the poles as well[R4-2][R4-40].
  • Ground-station contacts. Turn on the ground-station layer, move the point of interest to Nemea (37.82, 22.66) and read the contacts per day; the same tool answers the downlink-opportunity question on the ground segment page.

Model limits

What this simulator does and does not model

Orbits are circular with secular J2 drift of RAAN and argument of latitude (adequate for design-level revisit; no drag, no eccentricity, no manoeuvres). Satellites are visible from a ground point when the geometric elevation exceeds the mask — the footprint is a pure minimum-elevation cone with no antenna pattern, sensitivity or terrain. Every satellite is assumed to be collecting at all times, with no duty-cycle, power, storage or tasking constraints and no downlink latency; see collection planning for those. Cluster satellites are placed by along-track phase and RAAN offsets, so the cross-track separation varies with latitude and is not the J2-invariant oscillating formation that HawkEye flies[R3-1][R3-2]. Grid statistics are sampled at the chosen time step, so gaps shorter than one step are not resolved and cells seen only once report the full duration as their gap.
