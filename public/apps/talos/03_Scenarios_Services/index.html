03 Scenarios & services · Concept of operations

Concept of operations

TALOS is sold as a service, not as a satellite: customers hand over an area of interest and a frequency plan, the system turns the scarce minutes of collection per orbit into detections, geolocations, catalogue updates and alerts, and the loop closes by cueing other sensors. This page states the mission, draws the operational loop, walks through one real day of TALOS-1 passes over the Aegean computed for the baseline orbit, and sets out roles, operating modes, capacity, security tiers and what changes at each constellation stage.

Mission statement

What TALOS does, in one paragraph

TALOS passively monitors, characterises, geolocates and catalogues radio-frequency emitters from UHF (225 MHz) to X-band (12 GHz) over the Eastern Mediterranean, Aegean, Black Sea, Red Sea and Gulf approaches, starting with one 100–150 kg satellite at 530 km / 53° in 2030 and growing to a TDOA/FDOA constellation of 12–24 satellites by 2036. Its products are externals only — who is transmitting, where, when, with which signature — never content. Its value is a living EW database, an RF-signature catalogue and activity-pattern libraries that reveal abnormal behaviour, force-posture change and pre-kinetic indicators, and that cue SAR, EO, maritime and airborne ISR assets to the right place at the right time.

Three facts shape everything below. First, the customer buys outcomes (an alert that a jammer switched on near Odesa; a catalogue entry for a new S-band radar on an island; a track of a dark vessel) and does not care which satellite collected what. Second, the scarce resource is collection time: a satellite at 530 km sees a given area for 5–10 minutes per pass and can record only a fraction of its footprint and spectrum at once, so the collection manager's deck, not the receiver's sensitivity, decides what gets seen [R4-8]. Third, every documented commercial success is a persistent emitter or a correlation product — radars, jammers, fixed transmitters, RF-versus-AIS — because single-pass geolocation is coarse and multi-pass accumulation or fusion makes it useful [R4-7][R4-6]. The CONOPS is designed around these three facts.

The operational loop

The loop below is the service. Everything on the left is customer-facing; the satellite is one step among ten. The cycle time of the loop is the latency tier the customer pays for: hours in the standard tier (Greek ground site only), under 90 minutes with the KSAT Dubai/Puertollano complements, minutes once inter-satellite links carry on-board alerts (Stage 3+) — see Collection planning & data flow.

Two paths in the diagram deserve attention because they are where TALOS differs from a "collect everything and sort it out later" design. The dotted green path from the catalogue to the deck is the on-board signal-of-interest list: emitter reports are matched on the satellite against an uploaded signature library so that downlink priority and low-latency alerts can be decided in orbit, which is how a 1 Gbit/s link copes with a payload that would otherwise produce gigabits per second of raw IQ (research 03 §5: four 100 MHz channels at 12 bits are 9.6 Gbit/s before any reduction) [R3-26]. The feedback path on the left is the confirmation loop: a SAR image, an AIS match or a customer's ground truth promotes a catalogue entry from Provisional to Confirmed, exactly the mechanism by which HawkEye 360 and Airbus turned RF detections into a validated dark-fleet product off the Galápagos [R4-7]. The EW database page specifies both.

"Next Nemea contact" is the time from the end of the collection pass to the start of the next X-band pass over KSAT Nemea (37.82° N, 22.66° E) above 5°, i.e. the minimum data age for that pass in the standard latency tier. At 53° inclination Nemea and the Aegean are almost co-located, so most collections are downlinked on the same pass or the next orbit; collections over the Red Sea or the Gulf on the same orbit wait for the Dubai station.

What is collected on each pass

Why the passes bunch together

At 53° inclination the ascending and descending node crossings for a 38° N point fall into two groups of consecutive orbits roughly 12 hours apart, and the whole pattern drifts earlier by about four hours a day because the orbit plane precesses westward (about −3.8°/day at 530 km / 53°, computed from ORB.makeOrbit) while the Earth turns under it. Over a week every local hour is sampled, which is what pattern-of-life analytics need; on any single day there are gaps of 10–14 hours. A sun-synchronous alternative would give 3–5 passes/day at fixed local times (morning and evening) and no coverage of the other hours, which is why the baseline keeps the 53° plane for the regional mission and treats SSO as a launch-driven fallback (BASELINE.orbit.rationale).

Roles

The service is run by five roles. Headcount by phase is in Roadmap, cost & schedule (4–6 FTE operations for one satellite, 6–9 for three, analysts 5–8 from the cluster stage). HawkEye 360's prospectus warns that training and services compress margins when under-scoped, so the analyst layer is budgeted as a cost of goods, not overhead [R4-2].

Collection manager

Owns: the collection deck and the capacity ledger
  • Translates customer AOIs, priorities and revisit requirements into a per-orbit deck of tune/record windows
  • Deconflicts competing AOIs across satellites; enforces the ≈9 min/orbit budget
  • Runs pass and geometry prediction, publishes predicted CEP before accepting a task
  • Manages fixed-monitoring vs reserved vs on-demand queues

RF / ELINT analyst

Owns: the truth of every product that leaves the building
  • Validates detections and classifier output; resolves ambiguous geolocations (mirror solutions, multi-emitter overlaps)
  • Writes assessments — the Ukraine jamming report was analyst-produced [R4-6]
  • Characterises new emitters (PRI, PW, scan, modulation) for catalogue promotion
  • Handles customer questions about confidence and caveats

EW database manager

Owns: the catalogue, its maturity rules and the classified annex
  • Maintains signal classes, emitter instances, maturity transitions and audit trail
  • De-conflicts against national and NATO libraries; manages the one-way contribution flow
  • Controls release caveats per record and per customer
  • Curates the on-board signature library upload

Mission operations

Owns: spacecraft health, orbit and the downlink schedule
  • Platform telemetry, power and thermal limits during collection modes (124 W payload collecting, +45 W downlink)
  • Formation keeping in the cluster phase (150 km along-track, 5 km 1σ control)
  • Ground-station booking (KSAT Nemea primary, Dubai/Puertollano complements) and S-band tasking uplink from the owned Athens-area station
  • Anomaly response, software uploads, collision avoidance

Security officer

Owns: accreditation, clearances and the air gap
  • Facility and personnel clearances (NATO SECRET / EU SECRET handling expected by EU buyers [R4-16])
  • Classified enclave accreditation; NIS2 compliance of the ground segment
  • Export-control licensing of IQ, libraries and tasking rights to non-EU customers
  • Audit log review and incident handling

Product & API engineering

Owns: the pipeline, the console and the interfaces
  • L0→L4 pipeline, geolocation solvers, classifier retraining
  • GeoJSON / OGC API Features / STANAG 4676 export and customer onboarding
  • Alert rules engine and dashboard (see the demo console)
  • Fusion adapters: AIS, ADS-B, SAR/EO tasking APIs

Operating modes

HawkEye 360 distinguishes fixed monitoring, reserved and on-demand tasking [R4-2]; Kleos sold capacity by percentage, taskability and exclusivity [R4-8]. TALOS adopts the same three modes and binds them to the capacity ledger so that a sale is always a sale of minutes.

Capacity: the minutes ledger

Kleos quantified the constraint that matters: about 9 minutes of VHF collection per orbit per satellite, roughly 70 minutes per day, "heavily AOI-dependent" [R4-8]. For TALOS the same order of magnitude follows from three limits — the visibility time of the regional AOIs (5–10 minutes per pass), the payload power budget (124 W collecting against a 540 W end-of-life array with eclipses), and the daily data budget (≈30 GB/day of reports and snapshots through a 1 Gbit/s X-band link over 4–7 Nemea passes). Security and classification tiers

The architecture keeps two lines separated from the first day because the EUSPA RFI asks explicitly about classified handling and National Security Authority clearances [R4-16], European MoDs prefer processing within European borders [R4-23], and HawkEye 360 needed more than 200 export licences and a half-cleared workforce to serve the same markets [R4-2].

Design consequence

The catalogue schema must allow a classified annex to attach to an unclassified core record without changing the core (see EW database — classified annex). If the core schema carries classified fields, the commercial line cannot be exported and the business case collapses to a single national customer — the Kleos failure mode of too few buyers [R1-31].

The step that changes the business is Stage 1 → Stage 2: a single satellite sells monitoring (presence and intensity maps, spectrum occupancy, radar presence, catalogue seeding, EUSPA/EDF demonstrations), while the first cluster sells geolocation (confidence ellipses, dark-vessel correlation, jammer positions) and qualifies for EUSPA Part A and MoD air-defence monitoring of the USD 75 M class [R4-22]. The independent CYGNSS multi-satellite study found the same: the largest marginal gain in detection latency and revisit for GNSS-interference hotspots comes from going from one to three satellites (median detection latency 3 h 46 min → 48 min across 1 → 7 satellites, with most of the gain early) [R4-17]. The single-satellite vs constellation page quantifies the step; the coverage simulator and the TDOA/FDOA simulator let you change the assumptions.
