Collection planning & data flow

How a customer's request becomes minutes on the satellite and how bits on the satellite become an alert on the customer's screen. The page specifies the tasking workflow and its acceptance test (passes, geometry and predicted CEP computed before a task is accepted), the deconfliction rules for the ≈9 minutes of collection per orbit, the L0→L4 pipeline with its formats and latencies, the ground-station contact strategy for the TALOS orbit computed live for Nemea, Svalbard, Dubai and Puertollano, the resulting latency tiers, and the fusion and cueing interfaces with AIS, ADS-B, SAR/EO and ground passive sensors.

Tasking workflow

HawkEye 360 distinguishes fixed monitoring, reserved and on-demand tasking [R4-2]; Kleos quantified the capacity constraint (≈9 min per orbit per satellite) [R4-8]. TALOS binds the two: every task is scored for feasibility against the pass geometry it will actually get and against the minutes it will consume before the customer is told yes. The acceptance step is the part competitors do not expose — EUSPA's RFI asks explicitly for time-to-localise-and-notify and single- vs multi-satellite accuracy [R4-16], and a tasking interface that shows predicted CEP and pass times before committing answers the question honestly.

Feasibility before acceptance: passes, geometry and predicted CEP

The panel below is the acceptance test as the customer would see it. It propagates the baseline orbit (and the Stage-2 cluster of three satellites 150 km apart along-track, ±20 km cross-track) over the next 24 hours from the reference epoch, lists the passes over the selected AOI, and computes the thermal-noise-plus-clock CRLB ellipse for the best pass using the emitter class's bandwidth and a 100 ms snapshot at 10 dB SNR (research 03 §6.2, Stein form) [R3-2]. Clock (20 ns) and ephemeris (5 m) errors are added in quadrature; FDOA assumes a 0.05 Hz oscillator floor. Stage 1 figures come from the baseline planning values (5–20 km single pass, 1–5 km multi-pass on fixed emitters) because single-satellite accuracy is dominated by factors the CRLB does not capture (mirror ambiguity, emitter frequency stability, attitude knowledge).

Radars and jammers are accepted down to 5° because their EIRP makes SNR irrelevant (research 03 §1); communications classes need the elevation you set. SNR uses the class's typical EIRP from EMITTERS, the baseline antenna gain for its band (helix/survey 5–10 dBi at UHF/L, arrays 15–25 dBi at S/C/X), 2.5 dB noise figure and the class bandwidth. FDOA is only credited for classes whose emitters hold frequency (radars, fixed-frequency comms, SATCOM); frequency-hopping nets and cheap jammers get TDOA only.

Deconfliction rules

The collection manager runs a ledger per satellite per orbit. The rules below are the ones the planning software applies automatically; anything they cannot resolve goes to the human at Gate B.

  1. Budget. Collection time per orbit ≤ 9 min baseline (power and data), raised to 12 min when the downlink forecast for the next 24 h exceeds the data forecast by 30% (see the capacity calculator in the CONOPS).
  2. Sharing first. Two tasks whose AOIs fall in the same footprint and whose frequency plans fit the same channel configuration are merged into one collection; both customers are billed, one set of minutes is spent. This is the economics of HawkEye's regional subscriptions [R4-13].
  3. Channel fit. Four tuner channels and the RFSoC path are allocated by band; a task that needs a fifth simultaneous comms channel waits for the next pass or is time-sliced within the dwell (each slice ≥ 20 s so that Doppler fits remain possible).
  4. Priority pre-emption. P1 displaces P3/P4 until T−2 h; P2 reserved minutes are guaranteed but return to the pool at T−12 h if the customer has not placed an AOI.
  5. Geometry gate. A pass is only charged to a task if the predicted CEP (Stage 2) or the elevation (Stage 1) meets the acceptance shown at Gate A; otherwise it is not scheduled and the customer is not billed.
  6. Downlink awareness. Snapshot-heavy tasks (IQ tier) are scheduled on orbits that end at a ground contact, so that their data do not age behind lighter products.
  7. Self-interference. X-band receive is blanked during the satellite's own X-band downlink (BASELINE payload band note), so X-band radar tasks are not scheduled on downlink passes over Nemea unless the contact is S-band only.
  8. Formation (Stage 2). The cluster executes the same deck; the lead satellite's deck is authoritative and followers inherit it with a time offset equal to the along-track separation (≈20 s for 150 km).

The L0→L4 data pipeline

The pipeline follows what the operators practise (04 §4.3) and what the payload design forces: raw IQ cannot be downlinked (four 100 MHz channels at 12 bits are 9.6 Gbit/s, two orders of magnitude above the link), so reduction to pulse descriptors, signal reports and selected snapshots happens on board (research 03 §5) [R4-29]. Each level has a defined format and a latency contribution; the sum is the latency tier.

Contact strategy that follows from the numbers

At 53° the polar sites never see the satellite; Nemea alone gives about — contacts a day but a worst gap of about — hours, because the Earth rotates the ground track away for half a day. Adding Dubai and Puertollano — both at latitudes the 53° orbit crosses on every revolution — brings the network to roughly — contacts a day with a worst gap of about — hours (computed). On SSO the picture inverts: Svalbard alone sees — contacts a day and the worst wait is one orbit for most collections, but the regional revisit falls to 3–5 passes a day. The baseline therefore books Nemea as primary, Dubai and Puertollano as contracted complements for the NRT tier, and keeps the S-band TT&C station near Athens for tasking and alert flags; Svalbard and Troll are only in the plan if the first launch forces an SSO ride. Per-pass GSaaS pricing (€40–250 per pass, est.) makes this a few hundred thousand euro per year per satellite (research 05 §1.2).

Fusion and cueing

AIS and ADS-B

Every L2 geolocation at sea or in the air is joined against cooperative-tracking data before it becomes a product: ship radar fixes against AIS (MMSI, position within the ellipse at ±60 min, as in the Galápagos analysis [R4-7]), IFF/Link-16 and AEW fixes against ADS-B (ICAO24). HawkEye added MMSI matching to RFGeo in 2019 [R4-14]; Windward infers jamming from AIS corruption without any RF and would consume a source layer [R4-19]; ADS-B-based maps (EASA, gpsjam) show the effect of GNSS interference on receivers and TALOS adds the emitter position [R4-18]. The join produces three outcomes: matched (identity attached, confidence raised), unmatched (dark-vessel or non-cooperative candidate), inconsistent (AIS position outside the ellipse — spoofing or AIS error flag). Greece's own AIS/VDES receivers on the national EO microsatellites and terrestrial AIS are the natural feeds [R4-25].

SAR / EO cueing — the Galápagos template

The 2020 HawkEye 360–Airbus collection over the Galápagos EEZ remains the reference workflow: RF detections of marine radars and VHF not matching AIS defined the search box, a SAR collection found 58 vessels of which only 16 matched AIS within ±60 min, and the two layers together produced the dark-fleet picture [R4-7]. Galwan (2020) is the land analogue: an RF surge tasked EO imagery that showed the build-up [R4-14]; Spire exposes it as a "trigger third-party imagery" action [R4-9]. For TALOS the partner is in Athens: ICEYE Hellas SAR and the Open Cosmos optical microsats of the national programme [R4-25].

Ground passive sensors (ERA-style)

HawkEye 360's European strategy pairs its space RF with ERA's ground-based passive emitter tracking [R4-23]; the model for TALOS is integration with the Hellenic Air Force's passive surveillance over the Aegean. Ground passive systems give continuous, metre-class tracks of airborne emitters inside their coverage; the satellite gives coverage where there are no ground sensors (sea, foreign territory) and the parametric library that both can share. The interface is a two-way exchange of STANAG 4676 tracks and emitter parametric records in the classified line, and a one-way flow of cueing geometry from the ground network into the TALOS deck (which sector an AEW platform is working in, so the satellite dwells there).

Human-in-the-loop points

  • Gate A — task acceptance when predicted CEP or dwell is below the customer's minimum, or the request touches an export/legal flag.
  • Gate B — deck signature at T−2 h: the collection manager signs every deck; conflicts the rules could not resolve are decided here.
  • L3 promotion: an analyst confirms every catalogue transition to Confirmed or Characterised and every new signal class (see EW database).
  • Alert release for customers who require human release (default for MoD alerts; automated for GNSS-interference feeds to aviation/maritime platforms with a confidence threshold).
  • Cue approval when a SAR/EO tasking spends the customer's imagery budget.
  • Classified annex edits: only cleared staff, four-eyes principle, audit-logged.

Interfaces and formats

Commercial operators deliver GeoJSON and JSON over REST [R4-13][R4-8]; none advertises native STANAG 4676 tracks or a 4559 catalogue, while NATO ISR interoperability relies on exactly those [R4-44]. TALOS offers both from the start because the anchor customers are NATO and EU bodies.
