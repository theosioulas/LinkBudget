How to read the verdicts

Every use case is only as good as the weakest emitter it relies on. The chips under each card come from EMITTERS and carry the detectability verdict derived in the payload note for the TALOS baseline antennas and receiver (5–10 dBi at UHF/L, 15–25 dBi at S/C/X, 2.5 dB noise figure, 1,000–1,500 km slant range): yes means a comfortable link budget, marginal means detection depends on dwell, integration or a cue, and no means below the thermal floor for any aperture that fits a 150 kg bus. Click a chip to open the emitter's entry in the target emitters page. Three verdicts shape the whole portfolio: consumer drone links and 10 mW personal GNSS jammers are not detectable from LEO, and C/X-band VSAT sidelobes are marginal without the reflector option — so TALOS sells "UAS activity" (ground control stations, military links), "GNSS interference" (≥10–100 W jammers) and "uplink interference hunting" (with cueing and long dwell), and never claims more (research 03 §1 takeaways).

The stage on each card is the first stage at which the product is credible enough to charge for: Stage 1 (one satellite, 2030) sells monitoring and presence; Stage 2 (3-satellite cluster, 2032) sells geolocation with confidence ellipses; Stage 3 (three clusters, 2034) sells tracks, pattern-of-life and alerts; Stage 4 (12–24 satellites, 2036) sells persistence and latency SLAs. The mapping follows the service-tier logic of the use-case research (04 §6.3) and is consistent with how HawkEye 360 and Kleos staged their own offers [R4-13][R4-8].

Regional scenarios

The five scenarios below are the operational core of the business case. Each is written for the same spacecraft and the same reference orbit, and each says explicitly what one satellite sees on a single pass, what the three-satellite cluster adds, which RF indicators precede kinetic action, and which other ISR asset the RF product cues. They are scenarios, not reports: the emitter placements are representative, the documented events they rest on are cited.
