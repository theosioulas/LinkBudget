03 Scenarios & services · Single satellite vs constellation

Single satellite vs constellation

One TALOS satellite is a monitoring instrument: it sees a mid-latitude area of interest a handful of times a day, characterises what transmits there and can place persistent emitters to a few kilometres after several passes. The first three-satellite cluster is a different product class: instantaneous TDOA/FDOA fixes with confidence ellipses on single bursts. More clusters then buy revisit, not accuracy. This page quantifies each step with live simulations for an Athens-area AOI and sets out what to sell at each stage.

Baseline: TALOS-1Sources: research notes 03 §6, 04 §6Simulations: orbit.js (circular + J2)
Bottom line

The step from one satellite to the first cluster changes what can be sold (geolocation with ellipses instead of presence maps); every later step changes how often. Because a cluster counts as a single revisit node, nine satellites flown as three clusters give the revisit of three satellites, not nine. The programme should therefore treat the Stage 2 cluster as the real product launch and design TALOS-1 so that it is the first member of that cluster rather than a stand-alone demonstrator.

Revisit arithmetic for a mid-latitude AOI

A 530 km circular orbit has a period of — minutes, so a satellite completes — orbits a day while the Earth turns —° of longitude under each one. The visibility circle at 5° minimum elevation has a radius of — km (— km at 10°), so the ground tracks of two or three consecutive orbits pass within the circle of a point at 38°N on each side of the day. That is the whole story of single-satellite revisit: a group of 2–4 passes about 95 minutes apart, then a gap of 10–14 hours until the orbit plane comes round again on the other side of the Earth. For a sun-synchronous orbit the two groups are the fixed local-time morning and evening crossings; for the baseline 53° orbit they drift through the day over about two months[R4-8].

Two independent data points anchor the scaling. The CYGNSS GNSS-interference study used seven satellites in one inclined plane (±38° latitude) and measured a median revisit on the top-50 interference hotspots of 23 h with one satellite and 1 h 49 min with seven, with detection latency falling from 3 h 46 min to 48 min; the largest marginal gain was from one to three satellites[R4-17]. HawkEye 360 claims global revisit under 45 minutes with more than 30 satellites in 13–15 tri-satellite clusters across sun-synchronous and 40–46° planes[R4-2][R4-40]; Kleos promised daily revisit of any AOI with 3–4 clusters[R4-8]. Our simulations below reproduce this scaling with the TALOS baseline orbit.

Geolocation: one satellite vs a cluster

Revisit is only half the comparison. The product that customers pay most for — a position with a confidence ellipse on a single transmission — needs simultaneous measurements from several receivers. One satellite can only substitute time for receivers: it watches the emitter change position in its own frame as it flies past. The four geometries below are the complete menu; the TDOA/FDOA simulator computes the cluster cases exactly.

A note on ellipse orientation

Research note 03 §6.2 paraphrases Leiter & Gurfil as "error degrades along the formation's ground-track direction and is best perpendicular to it"[R3-11]. The Cramér–Rao computation in orbit.js gives a more specific rule. A pair's TDOA gradient is the baseline projected across the line of sight: the component of the baseline transverse to the ground line of sight counts in full, the component along it only in proportion to sin²ε. The weak axis of a TDOA fix therefore points from the emitter towards the formation. For an emitter abeam of an along-track formation — the closest-approach geometry of every cluster pass — that direction is cross-track, so the 95 % ellipse is elongated perpendicular to the track and shrinks only when the middle satellite carries a cross-track offset of 10–50 km, which is exactly what HawkEye's out-of-plane oscillation and Kleos' two-plane diamond provide[R3-1][R3-10]. For an emitter ahead of or behind the formation along the track the weak direction is along-track and the published sentence applies. The simulator shows both as its time slider moves the cluster past the emitter; the research note should state which geometry it refers to.

Decision guidance

What to sell at each stage

  • Stage 1 — one satellite: sell monitoring, not geolocation: GNSS-interference presence and intensity maps per pass, spectrum occupancy by band, radar-presence indicators, catalogue seeding for the national EW database, and demonstrations for EUSPA and the EDF. Price as AOI subscriptions with daily cadence, the model HawkEye used for its 2020 Regional Awareness Subscription[R4-13]. Any geolocation claim should be stated as "to the nearest 5–20 km, improving for persistent emitters", with the Doppler and AOA products labelled experimental.
  • Stage 2 — first cluster: add geolocation products with 95 % ellipses, dark-vessel correlation with AIS, GNSS jammer and spoofer positions; qualify for the EUSPA space-based GNSS-interference monitoring service[R4-16] and for national MoD air-defence monitoring of the kind HawkEye sells to a European ministry for up to USD 75 M[R4-22].
  • Stage 3 — three clusters: pattern-of-life and alerting; several fixes a day on the same emitter make tracks and STANAG 4676 products possible; Luno-style analytics subscriptions.
  • Stage 4 — 12–24 satellites: persistence SLAs by latitude band, tactical latency tiers (ISL plus polar or regional downlink), sovereign dedicated-capacity sales.

Why the first cluster matters more than the tenth satellite

Three arguments, all visible in the simulations above. First, the product discontinuity: the cluster unlocks the geolocation tier, which is where HawkEye's revenue sits (geolocation and knowledge products on top of detections; USD 117.7 M in 2025[R4-2]), while a single satellite can only sell presence maps. Second, the marginal revisit curve is steepest at the start: the CYGNSS study found the biggest gain between one and three nodes[R4-17], and our Athens simulation shows the maximum gap falling from about — h with one node to about — h with three clusters, then to — min with eight; the tenth satellite buys minutes, the third buys hours. Third, cost: the demonstrator is budgeted at €— M and the cluster at €— M (BASELINE.cost), so the cluster roughly doubles the money and changes the product class, while the 12-satellite stage at €— M only changes cadence. The practical consequence for TALOS-1 is that its clock, GNSS raw-observable output, snapshot time-stamping and formation-keeping ΔV must be specified for cluster operation from the start, so that satellites two and three complete a cluster rather than start a new design.

What the simulations do not model

Circular orbits with J2 secular drift, no drag, footprints as pure minimum-elevation cones, no duty cycle or tasking conflicts, no downlink latency, no sensitivity limit (an emitter in the footprint is counted as seen). Useful RF geometry — an emitter within about 1,000 km ground range for acceptable geolocation — reduces the counted passes to the 2–4 "useful" passes quoted in research note 04 §6.1. The coverage simulator lets you change elevation mask, orbit and constellation; the collection planning page covers tasking and data flow.
