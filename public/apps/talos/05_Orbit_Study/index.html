What more satellites buy — and what they don't

Typical revisit gap versus number of nodes (a node is a single satellite or a cluster flying as one unit) for Walker-style constellations at 53° and sun-synchronous inclination. The curve flattens quickly: the first three or four nodes remove most of the waiting; after about twelve, each additional node buys minutes, not hours. Irregularities come from the number of planes and the phasing chosen for each count.

Reading the curve for a cluster constellation

If you fly clusters, multiply the node count by three to get satellites: eight nodes of instantaneous geolocation are 24 satellites, while eight single satellites give the same revisit for detection-only products. That factor of three is the whole commercial trade between the Unseenlabs model (many singles, Doppler geolocation) and the HawkEye model (clusters, TDOA/FDOA).

Rules of thumb

  1. Latitude sets the pass count; inclination sets where the peak is. A satellite spends most of its time near its inclination latitude, so a 53° orbit gives 38°N almost twice the passes of a sun-synchronous orbit and nothing above 60°N. For Greece, the Black Sea and the Middle East, 45–55° is the right family; SSO is for global catalogues and polar downlink.
  2. Planes buy revisit; satellites in the same plane buy repetition. Two satellites 180° apart in one plane halve the mean gap but leave the same 13 h daily hole, because both cross the region at the same local times. Spreading nodes over planes fills the day.
  3. A cluster is one node. Three satellites flying 150 km apart give geolocation on every pass but no more passes. Decide early whether the product is "where is it now" (clusters) or "what is active, how often" (singles).
  4. The 15° mask is the honest one for geolocation. Low-elevation passes stretch footprints to 2,000 km and path loss by 12 dB; they count for detection but produce poor ellipses. Plan geolocation revisit on the 15° numbers.
  5. Beyond ~12 nodes you are buying minutes. From 165 min (one node) to 27 min (six nodes) to 16 min (twelve) at 38°N: the first six nodes deliver 85% of the achievable improvement. Spend the rest on a second inclination, inter-satellite links or ground stations before buying node thirteen.
  6. Downlink gaps can exceed revisit gaps. At 53° a single Greek ground station is unseen for 13–14 h a day. Revisit over the target is useless if the data wait on board; pair mid-inclination constellations with Mediterranean and Gulf stations or an ISL.

Sources and method

    Method: each template's satellites are propagated for three days from the common epoch; access windows above the elevation mask at the representative point are merged across all nodes; gaps are the intervals between windows. Figures are design-level (no drag, circular orbits, footprint = elevation cone) and differ from a mission-analysis tool by a few percent, not by factors. HawkEye 360's "< 45 min global" claim with 30+ satellites and the CYGNSS 1→7 satellite study are the external anchors.
