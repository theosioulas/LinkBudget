Inclination: where the passes are

A satellite spends most of its time near the latitude equal to its inclination, where the ground track turns around. The chart counts passes per day at a 5° mask for a single satellite at 530 km as a function of latitude, for six inclinations. Read your region's latitude and pick the inclination whose curve peaks there — but not so high that the plane's nodes spend their time elsewhere.

Why 530 km

At 530 km the 5° footprint already reaches 2,000 km, every extra 100 km buys only ~10% more access minutes but costs 0.8 dB of signal, a higher dose and tens of m/s of extra disposal ΔV. 500–550 km is also the shell where Transporter and Bandwagon insert, which keeps launch options open. For a cluster, lower altitude also tightens the TDOA error ellipse (dilution scales with range over baseline).

Planes: filling the day

Two satellites 180° apart in the same plane (template T3) double the looks but leave the daily hole untouched: both cross Greece during the same hours. Two single clusters in planes 90° apart (T6) give fewer looks but cut the worst gap from 13.7 h to 8 h; three planes (T7) bring it to ~7 h and the typical gap to 46 min. Getting into several planes costs either one launch per plane or time: at 530 km the J2 nodal drift differs by about 0.6° per day per 10 km of altitude difference, so a satellite parked 20 km higher for 75 days drifts ~90° in RAAN relative to its siblings — a free but slow way to build planes from one rideshare, used by Planet, Spire and HawkEye.

Cluster geometry (when the node is three satellites)

Along-track spacing L

150 km in the baseline (HawkEye 125–250 km). Longer baselines shrink the error ellipse along the baseline (dilution ≈ range/L) but shrink the common footprint and break co-visibility at low elevation; 50–250 km is the practical window at 500 km.

Cross-track offset W

±20 km in the baseline (HawkEye 20 km peak-to-peak oscillation). Without it a collinear formation is degenerate for emitters under its track — the ellipse along the weak axis grows with range/W. The TDOA/FDOA simulator shows this directly.

Control

±5 km (1σ) formation control with FEEP; ~60 m/s over three years. J2-invariant relative orbits keep the formation from drifting apart; differential drag is the backup.

Four-satellite diamond

Two planes ~1° apart (Kleos). Ambiguity-free fixes without FDOA and one spare, at the cost of cross-track control and a fourth spacecraft per node.

Ground stations are part of the orbit choice

Mid-inclination orbits lose the polar-station advantage: KSAT Nemea alone sees the 53° orbit ≈ 7–8 times a day in two groups and nothing for 13–14 hours. The ground segment page computes contacts for every baseline station; the short version is that a regional constellation needs Mediterranean (Nemea, Puertollano), Gulf (Dubai) and ideally an Atlantic or Asian station to keep data latency under 90 minutes, or an inter-satellite link.

Decision checklist for Phase A

  1. Confirm the anchor customer's area of interest; if it is within 30–55° latitude, baseline 53° (or 45° if Bandwagon's insertion is to be used without a plane change).
  2. Book two launch options: one Bandwagon (mid-inclination) and one Transporter (SSO) — the spacecraft must be designed for both beta-angle regimes.
  3. Fix 500–550 km; verify disposal ΔV against the thruster's total impulse (≈ 100 m/s low-thrust lowering to 350 km).
  4. Decide node type with the business plan: clusters if geolocation products are the revenue, singles if monitoring subscriptions are.
  5. Plan the ground network for the chosen inclination before the constellation grows past one node.
