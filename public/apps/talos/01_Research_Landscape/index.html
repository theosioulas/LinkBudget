01 Research & landscape · State of the art

State of the art in space-based passive RF

Space-based passive RF monitoring has moved in a decade from a handful of classified 500-kg national satellites to a commercial market led by clusters of 13–30 kg CubeSats, and in 2025–26 the leading operators and primes have moved back up to the 150 kg class to get aperture, power and X-band. That 150 kg class is the one TALOS targets: this page explains what the discipline is, how the architectures differ in the way they geolocate an emitter, who flies what, and where a Greek/EU entrant can stand.

What the discipline is

A passive-RF satellite carries no transmitter for its mission; it listens. Wideband receivers tuned between a few tens of MHz and 18 GHz record emissions from radars, data links, satellite phones, push-to-talk radios, VSAT terminals and GNSS jammers, and the on-board or ground processing extracts externals: frequency, bandwidth, pulse or burst timing, modulation class, power and, from the arrival time and Doppler shift at one or several satellites, the position of the emitter. Content is not demodulated; this is what distinguishes the commercial "RF geolocation" business of HawkEye 360, Unseenlabs and Kleos from classical COMINT, and what makes an unclassified, exportable product possible.

Three products have emerged from this measurement chain. Spectrum mapping records which bands are occupied where (Aurora Insight's original business). Geolocation turns time-of-arrival (TOA) and frequency-of-arrival (FOA) measurements into a position with a confidence ellipse; HawkEye 360 measures TOA/FOA on orbit and solves the time-difference (TDOA) and frequency-difference (FDOA) problem on the ground, delivering 95 % confidence ellipses. Characterisation and cataloguing turns repeated detections into an emitter library with patterns of life, which is where the durable value sits for defence customers (research note 04 §5). The three stack: a satellite that cannot geolocate can still map, and a constellation that geolocates well enough, often enough, feeds a catalogue.

The physics forces one architectural choice before anything else. A single satellite can only geolocate by exploiting its own motion (Doppler history over tens of seconds) or by measuring angle of arrival with an interferometric antenna; both need the emitter to stay on for a while and both yield kilometre-class to tens-of-kilometre results on a single pass. Two or more satellites that see the same burst at the same instant can geolocate a burst of a few milliseconds by TDOA/FDOA, with accuracy that scales with signal bandwidth, SNR, baseline geometry and the time synchronisation between the satellites (tens of nanoseconds). This is why the whole field sorts itself into "formations" and "monosatellites" (Section 4), and why TALOS is designed to start as the latter and grow into the former.

The market-size figure deserves its warning tag. The analyst estimates come from report vendors whose methodologies are not published and whose "space-based RF mapping" definition includes services that do not exist yet. The hard number is HawkEye 360's audited revenue: $117.7 M in 2025 with a first net profit of $2.7 M, and $49.8 M in Q2-2026 alone (+87 % year on year). One trade report quoted $98.7 M for 2025; the prospectus figure is used throughout this study. Every other operator is an order of magnitude smaller: Unseenlabs targets €100 M revenue by 2029 from a 2024 base it does not disclose, Spire guides $75–85 M for all of 2026 across weather, aviation and RF, and Kleos booked €272 K in 2022, the year before it failed. The revealed commercial market in 2026 is therefore roughly $150–250 M a year, concentrated in one US company whose largest customer is the NRO (39 % of 2025 revenue).

Three generations of spacecraft

The mass class of a passive-RF satellite is a proxy for what it can hear. Antenna aperture sets sensitivity at C- and X-band, power sets how many receiver chains can run at once and for how long per orbit, and volume sets whether a deployable interferometer baseline or a reflector fits. The field has gone through a full cycle.

150 kg class — where TALOS sits

~150 kg ESPA-class, L/S/C/X capable
  • Unseenlabs BRO-31 Gen-2 (Jul 2026, Transporter-17): ~150 kg, U-Space/GomSpace platforms, L/S/C/X (and Ku) to 12 GHz, ~1 M km² per pass; BRO-32 second unit Oct 2026; ~20–40 planned
  • BAE Systems Azalea (Nov 2025): 3 × ~150 kg RF + 1 ICEYE SAR at ~560 km, onboard "Enhanced SDR" AI, self-funded
  • Aerospacelab trio (Mar 2024): HawkEye-style triangle on its own 100-kg-class bus line (mass n/d)
  • CASSIOPÉE (DGA, Sept 2026): Airbus S250 bus + Unseenlabs payload for 2032
  • TALOS-1: 530 km / 53°, ≈104 kg with margin inside a 150 kg allocation, UHF–X, single satellite first, 3-sat TDOA/FDOA cluster second

Why the move back up? Unseenlabs is explicit that Gen-2 exists to cover L, S, C and X up to 12 GHz and to see land and space emitters, not just ship radars, and that it is roughly ten times the mass of Gen-1. BAE Systems built Azalea at 150 kg to carry an ultra-wideband sensor and enough compute to run AI classification on board. HawkEye 360 went the opposite way with Block 3 (smaller, ~75 % cheaper, ~80 % of the signals) because its revisit problem is solved by numbers and its most lucrative signals are in UHF–S. The two trends are consistent: a cheap CubeSat sees strong signals below 6 GHz; an X-band radar catalogue or weak-signal collection needs aperture and power, and ESPA-class is the smallest bus that gives both. The CubeSat precedents also show that the TALOS 150 kg allocation is generous for a first unit, which is why the baseline closes at 87 kg dry + 20 % margin and leaves 35–45 kg for a reflector, propellant or a second digitiser.

Key findings for management

  1. The market is real but concentrated. One operator (HawkEye 360) accounts for most of the roughly $150–250 M/yr revealed commercial market; it has >30 satellites, a $292 M backlog and an uncapped NRO operational contract. Competing on volume in the US market is not an option for a Greek entrant; a sovereign, regional, government-anchored rationale is (see Market & regulation).
  2. Europe has no sovereign commercial RF layer and knows it. IISS ranks SIGINT/RF-mapping as Europe's most constrained ISR gap and costs 4–8 national SIGINT satellites at ~$200 M each. France is rebuilding (CASSIOPÉE 2032), Germany is competing SPOCK-2 with SIGINT sensors, the UK has one prime-funded cluster; HawkEye 360 is filling the gap from Luxembourg with Leonardo.
  3. The 150 kg class is the new consensus for multi-band (L→X) collection. Unseenlabs Gen-2, BAE Azalea and the Airbus S250-based CASSIOPÉE all sit there; it is the smallest class that carries X-band aperture and 100–200 W of payload power. TALOS is in the right class, and 2030 is late enough that the suppliers will have flown (research note 02).
  4. Single-satellite geolocation is now credible for persistent emitters (Unseenlabs since 2019, Spire 2026), which makes a one-satellite first stage sellable as monitoring; cluster TDOA/FDOA remains the only proven way to fix a short burst, which is why Stage 2 is a 3-satellite formation.
  5. Time to IOC is 4–6 years and the cost of a first operational cluster is €60–120 M for a 150 kg class including launch, ground and two years of operations (analyst inference, bracketed by Unseenlabs Gen-2 unit contracts ~€5 M bus-only and CERES at €450 M/3). Kleos shows what happens with less money and a 20-month commissioning slip.
  6. Every survivor is government-anchored: NRO 39 % of HawkEye revenue; Spire pivoting to defence after selling maritime; Unseenlabs embedded in a DGA programme. Pure commercial maritime demand did not sustain Kleos or Horizon.
  7. Nobody publishes independently verified accuracy. HawkEye gives 95 % ellipses without a CEP, Unseenlabs claims ~1 km, Kleos claimed 300 m. TALOS should publish predicted CEP with its conditions in the tasking interface — an honest differentiator rather than a weakness.
  8. Greece has the industrial precursors but no RF line. The €130 M national smallsat programme is EO-only; PHASMA (Libre Space + HSC) is a 3U RF-monitoring IOD; HAITF at Schimatari can test a 150 kg satellite; Open Cosmos Aegean builds in Pallini; KSAT operates Nemea. The RF layer is the missing modality in Hellas Space 2.0.

Where TALOS sits

Positioning statement

TALOS is a 150 kg-class, ITAR-free, UHF-to-X-band passive-RF microsatellite built and operated from Greece, flown first as a single monosatellite for monitoring, characterisation and catalogue seeding over the Eastern Mediterranean, Aegean and Black Sea, then as a 3-satellite TDOA/FDOA cluster for burst geolocation, and finally as a 12–24 satellite regional-persistence constellation. It competes with HawkEye 360 on sovereignty and regional revisit, not on global scale; it is the Greek/EU equivalent of what Unseenlabs is to France and Azalea is to the UK, with the X-band radar catalogue and the EU-sourced hardware chain as the technical differentiators.

Two things in that table are deliberate choices rather than inheritances. First, the 53° inclination: a sun-synchronous orbit is what Transporter rideshares deliver and what every commercial operator flew first, but mid-inclination planes give two to three times more passes over the Eastern Mediterranean, Black Sea and Middle East, which is exactly why HawkEye 360 added 40–46° planes and launches Clusters 15/16 on Bandwagon. Second, the dual-path receiver with an 8-channel 5 GSPS RFSoC: no operator discloses its digitiser, but HawkEye's choice of the Xiphos Q-series (Zynq) on 24 satellites and the Pathfinder's AD9361 + Zynq 7045 heritage show the family, and the 2 GHz instantaneous bandwidth per channel is what the X-band radar mission needs that a 56 MHz CubeSat SDR cannot give. The rest of this chapter supports these choices: the operator profiles, the supplier catalogue, the market and regulatory path, and the lessons learned from the companies that did it before.
