Market, customers and regulation

The bottom line: the revealed market for commercial space RF is about $150–250 M a year and is bought almost entirely by governments through study-then-operational contracts, with the EU's EUSPA GNSS-interference RFI and NATO's APSS the two European entry points; a Greek operator needs no new law to collect externals from orbit, but it needs a national licence under Law 4508/2017 with no precedent, an ITU filing through EETT thirty months before launch, an export classification that keeps the satellite ITAR-free and out of ML11, and a written legal opinion that the externals-only model stays outside the interception regime. All four must start in Phase A.

The analyst estimates are kept on the page because customers and investors will quote them, but they are flagged: methodologies are opaque, definitions elastic, and the two reports disagree by an order of magnitude on what counts. The number to plan against is the revealed one. HawkEye 360's $117.7 M (2025) and $215–220 M guidance (2026), Spire's $75–85 M across all lines, Unseenlabs' undisclosed revenue against a €100 M target for 2029, and Kleos's €272 K in its last full year put the space-segment commercial market at roughly $150–250 M in 2026, growing at 50–90 % a year at the leader. Capital markets are more generous than revenue: the HawkEye IPO priced at ~$2.4 B, about 20× 2025 revenue, Unseenlabs raised €85 M in 2024 and Spire $70 M in 2026, and European defence/dual-use start-ups raised a record $8.7 B in 2025 — appetite is strong but concentrated in the leader.

Demand is visible in the use cases that have been paid for (research note 04 §1): GNSS interference mapping (the Ukraine–Belarus jamming indicator published March 2022; the July 2025 GNSS-I suite; Windward's ~47,000 jammed vessels since Feb 2026; EASA's FIR rankings), dark-vessel detection (Galápagos 2020 with Airbus SAR), air-defence radar monitoring (the $75 M European MoD subscription), force-posture indicators (Galwan 2020), and interference hunting for a satellite operator (the NOAA GOES case, July 2026). GNSS interference is the product with the widest customer base and the simplest signal; it is the natural TALOS first product.

Customer landscape

SegmentWho buys, and what they have boughtProcurement entry point

Four features of this landscape shape the TALOS plan. First, the US route is long and well-defined — NRO ran RF studies from Sept 2022 to an uncapped operational contract in Aug 2026, about four years, and the next round is a Commercial Solutions Opening with a rolling five-year window; a non-US entrant can join it (Spire and Umbra are in it), but it requires US-releasable data and a US presence. Second, the NGA buys analytics rather than collections (Luno A/B, $490 M combined ceiling, delivery orders $3.5–24.4 M), which is where an RF-derived "activity change" product would be sold. Third, NATO has moved from owning assets to contracting services: the Alliance Persistent Surveillance from Space programme and its Aquila virtual constellation pool national and commercial assets through NCIA, which has issued a market opportunity for commercial ISR data from space; Greece can contribute a national asset. Fourth, the EU signal is specific: EUSPA's RFI on space-based GNSS RFI monitoring (deadline 27 Feb 2025) asked for a full service (Part A) or hosted payloads (Part B) on Galileo E1/E5a/E5b/E6 and GPS L1/L2/L5, with questions on update rate, time-to-localise-and-notify, single- versus multi-satellite geolocation, jamming/spoofing discrimination and National Security Authority clearances; EDF SPACE-ISR calls fund smallsat ISR constellations from CONOPS to prototype with SIGINT among eligible sectors; Frontex (EUROSUR) and EMSA already buy satellite maritime layers and Frontex has bought RF from HawkEye.

For Greece, Hellas Space 2.0 (€350 M, Aug 2026) covers VHR optical, thermal, data integration and workforce but does not name RF; the national posture pairs Open Cosmos optical microsats, ICEYE Hellas SAR assembly, a Planetek Hellas geospatial hub and HAI facility upgrades, and the ELIAMEP analysis recommends a joint tasking board including defence. The Hellenic MoD / HNDGS is the plausible anchor for the Aegean, Eastern Mediterranean and Black Sea approaches; the Hellenic Coast Guard, EETT (spectrum enforcement) and the satellite-operator interference-hunting niche widen the base. Japan (16 % of HawkEye revenue), the Gulf and India show that allied non-US governments pay for RF data when the delivery is sovereign-friendly.

Greek and EU regulatory path

National authorisation: Law 4508/2017 as amended by 4712/2020

Greece's national space law is Law 4508/2017 ("Licensing of space activities – Registration in the National Register of Space Objects"), amended by Law 4712/2020. Authorisation is granted by decision of the Minister of Digital Governance; conditions include adequate provision for debris mitigation and non-contamination, environmental-impact reports before launch, after launch and at end of life, and insurance at the Minister's discretion. The implementing Joint Ministerial Decision on report content and technical standards had not been adopted as of February 2025, so international and ESA standards apply in the interim. The Hellenic Space Center (HSC/ΕΛΚΕΔ, created 2019 under the Ministry) is the national agency and technical adviser and will assess the application and keep the National Register entry (UN Registration Convention). There is no Greek precedent for a passive-RF intelligence payload; allow 12–18 months from first contact to licence and engage HSC and the Ministry informally in Phase A. The licence fee is undefined; legal and compliance cost over Phases A–D is €0.2–0.5 M (est.).

Spectrum: EETT, ITU Coordination Request and Decision 482

BandsS-band TT&C 2025–2110 MHz up / 2200–2290 MHz down (Space Operations / EESS / Space Research); X-band 8025–8400 MHz EESS downlink. A passive RF-sensing mission is defensible as EESS; confirm the service classification with EETT early. The receive-only sensing bands need no ITU filing but get no protection.
ProcedureGreece notifies ITU through EETT (Hellenic Telecommunications & Post Commission) with the Ministry; national frequency assignment for TT&C and downlink runs in parallel. For non-GSO networks subject to coordination (Art. 9.11A bands) the API step was abolished: file a Coordination Request (CR/C) → coordinate with affected administrations → Notification (N) and bring into use within the 7-year regulatory period; for non-coordinated bands, API only.
TimelinePublication 4–6 months; coordination 12–36 months; file at PDR, 30 months before launch (2027-Q4 for a 2030-Q2 launch).
CostITU cost recovery under Council Decision 482 (as modified 2024): flat fee per filing plus per-unit charges, typically CHF 10–60 k for a non-GSO filing (est.); the administration may add its own fee (Ofcom's cost-recovery regime is the public example).
RiskMid-inclination orbits and 8 GHz X-band are congested with EO constellations; coordination delays and EPFD-type constraints are a schedule risk (T-15). Mitigation: standard EESS/SO bands, agile TT&C, EETT engagement in Phase A.

EU Space Act (proposal COM(2025) 335)

The Commission proposal of 25 June 2025 introduces EU-wide authorisation, cybersecurity/resilience and environmental-footprint obligations for operators. As of March 2026 the Council presidency had a compromise text (5 Dec 2025) and Parliament had proposed revisions (3 Mar 2026) with no agreement; application was proposed for 1 Jan 2030 (Commission) or 36 months after entry into force (Council, Parliament); authorisation would be handled by the Member State of main establishment with a 12-month (Council) or 6-month (Parliament, deemed approval) window; cybersecurity would be either a bespoke resilience chapter or full NIS2 applicability; fines up to 2 % of worldwide turnover; assets launched before application are grandfathered. Implication: TALOS-1 (2030) is likely grandfathered, the cluster (2032) is not. The ground segment is designed to NIS2 from day one and a debris/footprint file is kept from Phase B.

Debris and disposal

IADC guidelines and ISO 24113 set 25-year post-mission disposal; the FCC 5-year rule (adopted Sept 2022) binds US-licensed and US-market-access satellites launched after Sept 2024; ESA's Zero Debris approach requires ≤5 years disposal with ≥90 % success probability for ESA missions from 2030 and the Zero Debris Charter (2023) is widely signed; the draft EU Space Act codifies disposal and collision-avoidance duties; insurers reward Charter compliance with lower deductibles. At 500–550 km a 150 kg satellite with ~1 m² drag area decays in roughly 5–15 years uncontrolled (est.) — borderline. The baseline therefore reserves — m/s of the — m/s FEEP budget for disposal, passivates at end of life and subscribes to a conjunction-assessment service (EU SST or commercial).

Legal basis of externals-only collection

The system measures externals — frequency, bandwidth, modulation class, timing, power, direction, activity patterns — not the content of communications. This is the backbone of how HawkEye 360, Unseenlabs and Kleos operated commercially. The legal reasoning, which must be confirmed by a written opinion in Phase A (gate G0), is layered:

  • Greece. Article 19 of the Constitution protects the secrecy of correspondence and communications; lifting of secrecy is governed by Law 2225/1994 and Law 5002/2022 (the post-2022 reform with ADAE oversight), which address interception by state authorities, and by criminal-code provisions on unlawful interception. A commercial operator that does not demodulate or store content and treats emitters (radars, data links, jammers, AIS/VHF beacons) as physical phenomena operates outside the interception regime. Collection of content-bearing signals (satphone, HF/VHF voice, cellular) for anything other than technical characterisation would require either a state mandate under Law 3649/2008 (EYP) / 5002/2022 or a strict technical policy of immediate discard.
  • EU. ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC Art. 5 (confidentiality of communications over public networks) and the GDPR apply when data are personal; emitter metadata becomes personal data when it can identify a natural person (a small vessel's MMSI linked to an owner, a satphone terminal). Data-protection-by-design: pseudonymise identifiers, define retention, run a DPIA for any product touching civil communications.
  • ECtHR. Big Brother Watch v UK and Centrum för rättvisa v Sweden (Grand Chamber, 25 May 2021) accept bulk interception for national security with end-to-end safeguards; relevant if a Greek state customer tasks the system, and an argument for a clear tasking and oversight protocol in the government service agreement.
  • International law. Passive sensing from orbit is lawful under the Outer Space Treaty (freedom of use; Remote Sensing Principles 1986 by analogy); no state consent is needed to observe emissions that reach orbit.
Spire's voice product is not a TALOS product

Spire's 2025 RF service transcribes and translates unencrypted VHF/UHF/L-band voice. Under the Greek framework that is content collection and would need a state mandate; TALOS sells activity maps of push-to-talk and tactical links, never content (risk P-08, with technical enforcement and audit).

Classified handling and customers

Products for the Hellenic MoD and NATO attract classification (NATO RESTRICTED–SECRET; EU RESTRICTED–SECRET under Council Decision 2013/488/EU). The company needs a Facility Security Clearance from the Greek National Security Authority, cleared personnel, a segregated classified enclave (air-gapped processing; a SECRET-accredited ground link if tasking is sensitive) and an unclassified commercial product line in parallel; EUSPA's RFI explicitly asks about NSA clearances and classified handling, and HawkEye describes its platform as vetted for classified enclaves with nearly half its staff cleared. Sales to non-NATO customers need an EU dual-use or military export licence, end-user certificates and, if any US content, US re-export consent; product tiers are designed so that the most sensitive capabilities (specific radar fingerprints) are government-only. European MoDs favour processing, integration and security controls inside European borders — the reason HawkEye created a Luxembourg entity for localised data management; an EU sovereign cloud or accredited on-premises enclave is a selling point for TALOS, not a cost.

Decisions

D-1 · Start the four regulatory threads in Phase A (2027-Q1)

Legal opinion on externals-only collection; HSC/Ministry informal engagement for the Law 4508/2017 licence; EETT pre-coordination and ITU filing strategy; export classification (ITAR-free, EU DU vs ML11). All four are G0 go-criteria.

D-2 · File the ITU Coordination Request at PDR (2027-Q4)

X-band 8025–8400 MHz EESS and S-band SO/EESS TT&C through EETT, 30 months before launch; budget CHF 10–60 k ITU cost recovery plus national fees; keep TT&C agile for coordination outcomes.

D-3 · Frame TALOS as EESS / RF-environment monitoring in commercial documents

Externals-only, no content, with technical enforcement and audit; defence products through the national channel with a Facility Security Clearance; unclassified and classified lines in parallel.

D-4 · Design the ground segment to NIS2 and the EU Space Act from Phase B

TALOS-1 is likely grandfathered, the 2032 cluster is not; a debris/footprint file, ≥90 % disposal probability and propulsion reserved for disposal are built into the baseline now rather than retrofitted.

D-5 · Sell GNSS interference first, to EU and national buyers

EUSPA's RFI is the most concrete European demand signal and the simplest signal; EDF SPACE-ISR consortia and NATO APSS national contribution are the institutional routes; the Hellenic MoD anchor contract pays on milestones (P-01).
