02 Technical understanding · Geolocation

Geolocation theory

How a passive receiver in low Earth orbit turns a few hundred milliseconds of someone else's signal into a position on the ground — first with one satellite (angle, Doppler, repeated passes), then with the TALOS cluster (TDOA/FDOA). The bottom line: a single TALOS-1 delivers 5–20 km fixes per pass and 1–5 km on fixed emitters after several passes; three satellites 150 km apart deliver 0.3–3 km on narrowband comms and 100–500 m on radars, and the budget is dominated by thermal noise and geometry, not by the 20 ns clock.

Why geolocation is the product

Detection alone — "a 3 GHz radar is on somewhere in this 2,000 km footprint" — has limited value. The products customers pay for (L2 geolocations, L3 catalogue, L4 analytics) all hang off a position with a confidence ellipse: a jammer here, a frigate's radar on this track, a SAM battery moved 40 km since Tuesday. Everything in this chapter therefore ends in one number: the 95 % error ellipse (or CEP) the system can honestly promise for a given signal, geometry and stage of the constellation. The interactive geometry lives in the TDOA / FDOA simulator; this page is the theory behind what that simulator computes.

Two families of method exist. Single-satellite methods exploit the satellite's own motion or an antenna array and are what TALOS-1 can do alone from 2030. Multi-satellite methods compare the same signal received at two or more satellites and are what the 3-satellite cluster adds from 2032. The physics is the same in both cases — range, range-rate and angle to an emitter encoded in time, frequency and phase — but the error budgets are very different.

Single-satellite methods

(a) Angle of arrival by interferometry

Two antenna elements separated by a baseline \(D\) receive the same wavefront with a phase difference that depends on the angle \(\theta\) from the array boresight:

$$ \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi D}{\lambda}\sin\theta, \qquad \sigma_\theta = \frac{\lambda\,\sigma_\phi}{2\pi D\cos\theta} $$

\(\sigma_\phi\) is the residual phase error after calibration (radians); \(\sigma_\theta\) is the resulting bearing error.

Projected on the ground at slant range \(d\) and grazing elevation \(\varepsilon\) at the emitter, the cross-range error is \( \approx d\,\sigma_\theta \) and the along-line-of-sight error is \( \approx d\,\sigma_\theta / \sin\varepsilon \), so the error cell is an ellipse stretched away from the satellite[R3-2]. The TALOS DF array is a 1.2 m cross-boom carrying four sinuous elements (baselines 0.6 / 0.85 / 1.2 m) covering 1–12 GHz; the table below applies the equations to it with the 5° residual phase budget from the payload engineering note.

Three things follow. First, AOA is a kilometre-class method per snapshot and improves only as \(1/\sqrt{n}\) with \(n\) independent snapshots across a pass — twenty snapshots buy a factor 4–5[R3-2]. Second, the array is useless if the spacecraft does not know where it is pointing: 1 mrad of attitude error is 1 km of ground error at 1,000 km, which is why TALOS carries two star trackers for 0.02° knowledge (0.35 mrad) where HawkEye's Pathfinders flew with 2.1° pointing[R3-1]. Third, the 5° phase budget is a thermal and mechanical requirement: 4 mm of path mismatch is 5° at 1 GHz, hence the ±2 °C front-end plate, matched cables and the injected calibration tone in the component model. Phase wraps whenever \(D > \lambda/2\), so the long baseline alone is ambiguous; the array resolves this coarse-to-fine (short baseline for the unambiguous lobe, long baseline for precision) and by using three non-collinear baselines, the standard correlative-interferometer approach in ESM receivers.

(b) Doppler / frequency of arrival over a pass

A satellite at 7.3 km/s sees every ground emitter's carrier shifted by the projection of its velocity on the line of sight:

$$ f_r(t) = f_0\left(1 - \frac{\mathbf{v}_{sat}(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}(t)}{c}\right) + \delta f, \qquad \dot f \big|_{TCA} \approx -\frac{f_0}{c}\,\frac{v^2}{d} $$

\(\hat{\mathbf u}\) is the unit vector satellite → emitter, \(\delta f\) the unknown emitter frequency offset, \(d\) the slant range at closest approach.

At 1 GHz the shift swings through ±25 kHz over a pass and changes fastest at the time of closest approach: about 385 Hz/s at 500 km range, 193 Hz/s at 1,000 km[R3-13]. The curve's shape — how steep the zero crossing is and when it happens — encodes the emitter's along-track position (time of closest approach) and its cross-track distance (steepness: a far emitter sees a gentler S-curve). Fitting \(f_r(t)\) over 1–8 minutes with the unknowns (lat, lon, \(\delta f\)) and an Earth-surface constraint gives a fix; the chart below does exactly that for TALOS-1, propagating the baseline orbit and evaluating the geometric Doppler every 5 s.

The chart shows the method's two limitations directly. The solid and dashed curves for offset A are identical: the Doppler history is symmetric about the ground track, so a single pass cannot tell east from west — the mirror ambiguity. It is broken by the antenna pattern (a directional antenna looking one way), by an AOA measurement, or by a second pass with different geometry. And the difference between offsets A and B is only in the slope through zero; the fit therefore needs the emitter to hold its frequency to roughly \(10^{-8}\)–\(10^{-9}\) over the observation. Crystal-controlled radios, radars and fixed terminals do; cheap VCO jammers and frequency-hopping nets do not, and for those the method degrades to "somewhere along the ground track at this time". Sensitivity: position error ≈ \(\lambda\,\sigma_f\,d/v\) × a geometry factor of 1–10 — at 1 GHz and 1,000 km, about 40 m per hertz of frequency error before the geometry factor. Published results bracket what is achievable: Clements et al. fixed a stationary S-band GNSS spoofer to 68 m from a single 20 s Spire capture (6.7 km 95 % semi-major axis; simulated worst cases 2–24 km)[R3-14]; FoA interference-localisation studies find satellite velocity error and emitter oscillator offset (≈ 50 Hz bound) to be the dominant terms[R3-13]; Unseenlabs' whole Gen-1 product is single-satellite geolocation "to the nearest kilometre", almost certainly Doppler/multi-snapshot (inference)[R3-6].

(c) Multi-pass triangulation

Successive passes of one satellite are 95 minutes apart and the geometry rotates by 20–25° of longitude each time, so lines of bearing or Doppler fixes from several passes intersect at angles that make the solution well-conditioned. For a stationary emitter the error falls to the few-hundred-metre class over days — adequate for fixed radar sites, SATCOM terminals and permanent jammers, which is exactly the content of an EW order-of-battle database. For anything that moves between passes (ships at 10 m/s cover 57 km in 95 minutes) the method is useless, and that gap is the fundamental argument for the cluster. Multi-pass fusion is also what resolves the mirror ambiguity for free: the mirror image of pass 1 does not coincide with the mirror image of pass 2.

(d) TDOA against a known reference

If a cooperative or known emitter sits in the same footprint — an AIS base station, an ADS-B ground site, a calibration beacon the operator runs — the differential time of arrival between the reference and the target through the same receiver cancels the receiver's clock bias and most of its path delays. This is the single-satellite analogue of the gateway-reference scheme in the FoA literature[R3-13]. For TALOS it is a calibration tool rather than a product: it is how the commissioning campaign measures the timestamp latency and phase offsets of each chain, and how the 20 ns inter-satellite budget is verified in orbit against ground truth (risk T-03).

Multi-satellite methods: TDOA and FDOA

Two or more satellites receiving the same burst add two new observables. The time difference of arrival fixes the difference of ranges to the two satellites; the frequency difference of arrival fixes the difference of range-rates. Each has its own surface of position, and the emitter is where those surfaces meet the Earth.

TDOA hyperboloids

$$ \Delta\tau_{ij} = \frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}_i| - |\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}_j|}{c} + (b_i - b_j) $$

\(\mathbf r_i\) satellite positions, \(\mathbf x\) emitter, \(b_i\) receiver clock biases.

Constant range difference is a hyperboloid of two sheets with the satellites at its foci; intersected with the Earth's surface it is a curve. Two satellites therefore give a line of position (ambiguous along it); three give a point plus its mirror; three or four with FDOA, or a fourth satellite, remove the ambiguity[R3-2][R3-11]. The clock-bias term is why the 20 ns synchronisation matters: 1 ns is 30 cm of range difference, and a bias that is not common to both satellites maps directly into the fix.

FDOA iso-Doppler surfaces

$$ \Delta f_{ij} = \frac{f_0}{c}\left(\dot r_i - \dot r_j\right), \qquad \dot r_i = \mathbf{v}_i\cdot\frac{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}_i}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}_i|} $$

For two satellites 100 km apart along-track at 1,000 km range and 1 GHz the FDOA spans about 2.5 kHz across the footprint, a ground gradient of ≈ 2.5 mHz/m — or ≈ 400 m per hertz of FDOA error[R3-2]. The iso-Doppler contours run roughly perpendicular to the TDOA hyperbolae in the along-track direction, which is why FDOA is so valuable for a coplanar formation whose TDOA geometry alone is weak. The requirements it imposes are on frequency, not time: the two local oscillators must agree to ≪ 0.1 Hz at 1 GHz (\(10^{-10}\) relative) over the snapshot, which GNSS-disciplined OCXOs (\(10^{-11}\)–\(10^{-12}\) short-term) meet and free-running TCXOs do not; and the emitter itself must drift ≪ 0.1 Hz during the 100 ms snapshot (radars and fixed-frequency comms pass, frequency-hoppers fail).

Hybrid TDOA/FDOA: the cross-ambiguity function

In practice nobody measures TDOA and FDOA separately. The two time-stamped recordings are cross-correlated over a grid of trial delays and frequency offsets — the cross-ambiguity function (CAF) — and the peak gives both at once, with the peak's width in each axis giving the measurement uncertainties. Kleos' published pipeline is the clearest open description: time-align downlinked recordings, slice in time and frequency, compute pairwise CAFs, filter blind measurements, then solve by iterative non-linear least squares with a zero-altitude constraint and report a confidence ellipse[R3-10]. HawkEye combines TDOA/FDOA across its three-satellite cluster with GNSS-disciplined clocks and an S-band inter-satellite link so that the CAF can run on orbit[R3-1][R3-2]. TALOS adopts the Kleos path for the first cluster (ground CAF on downlinked snapshots, which is why every snapshot carries GNSS time, PVT and raw observables) and the HawkEye path when the ISL is added.

Thermal-noise accuracy (Stein form)

How well the CAF peak can be located is set by the signal's bandwidth and duration and by the effective SNR of the pair. In the form HawkEye uses[R3-2]:

$$ \sigma_\tau \approx \frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{B\,T\,\gamma}}, \qquad \sigma_f \approx \frac{1}{T_e\sqrt{B\,T\,\gamma}} $$ $$ \beta = 2\pi B_{rms},\quad B_{rms}=B/\sqrt{12}\ \text{(rectangular spectrum)},\quad T_e = 2\pi T/\sqrt{12} $$ $$ \gamma = \frac{1}{1/\gamma_1 + 1/\gamma_2 + 1/(\gamma_1\gamma_2)} $$

\(B\) noise/signal bandwidth (Hz), \(T\) integration time (s), \(\gamma_{1,2}\) the SNRs at the two satellites; \(BT\) is the time-bandwidth product. The Cramér-Rao bound for TDOA-only geolocation scales as \(1/(\eta\rho)\) with \(\eta = W_e^2/c^2\), improves as \(1/N\) with \(N\) satellites and grows roughly as \(h^2\) with altitude[R3-12].

The consequence is counter-intuitive for people used to radar: a weak but wideband signal is easier to locate than a strong narrowband one. A 25 kHz voice channel integrated for 100 ms at 10 dB SNR gives \(\sigma_\tau\) of the order of 100 ns (30–40 m of range difference); a single 1 µs radar pulse with 1 MHz bandwidth at 20 dB gives ≈ 55 ns, and a hundred such pulses coherently combined give ≈ 1.6 m[R3-2]. Geometric dilution: GDOP ≈ d / L

The hyperbolae and iso-Doppler contours in the figure above are only steep near the formation; far along-track they flatten and small measurement errors slide the intersection a long way. The order-of-magnitude rule is \( \text{ground error} \approx \text{GDOP} \times \sigma_d \) with \( \text{GDOP} \approx d/L \) for range \(d\) and baseline \(L\) — exactly, the covariance follows from the Jacobian \(H\) of the measurements with respect to emitter position, and the CEP from projecting the error ellipsoid onto the local tangent plane[R3-2]. For d = 1,000 km and L = 100 km, GDOP ≈ 10: the 25 kHz comms case becomes ≈ 400 m and radars 20–160 m before clock, ephemeris and propagation terms. Error is worst along the formation's ground-track direction and best perpendicular to it[R3-11]. The TALOS cluster's 150 km spacing (GDOP ≈ 3.5 at nadir, ≈ 7 at 1,000 km) is a deliberate middle: larger baselines reduce GDOP but shrink the common footprint and break co-visibility at low elevation.

Planning figures per constellation stage

Sell the ellipse, not a number

Every product should carry its predicted 95 % ellipse computed from the actual pass geometry, signal bandwidth and SNR (the simulator does this with ORB.geoAccuracy). Promising a fixed CEP is how operators have lost credibility: the same cluster that fixes a 1 MHz radar to 200 m will place a 25 kHz push-to-talk radio at 3 km on a poor-geometry pass. Risk T-07 is managed by this rule and by a calibration campaign against known emitters in commissioning.

Design consequences for TALOS

  • Timing unit as a core item. GNSS-disciplined OCXO ≤ 20 ns with CSAC holdover, hardware timestamp counter latched at the ADC, PPS fan-out to every digitiser, and raw GNSS observables logged with every snapshot. This is what makes Stage 2 TDOA and FDOA (\(10^{-11}\) frequency agreement) possible.
  • Star-tracker ADCS. 0.02° attitude knowledge for the AOA product; otherwise the 1.2 m DF boom is dead mass.
  • Phase-stable DF chain. ±2 °C plate, matched phase-stable harness, injected calibration tone — a 5° phase budget at 1 GHz is 4 mm of path.
  • Snapshots as the unit of product. 20 kB comms / 1 MB radar snapshots with time, PVT and attitude, stored and downlinked for ground CAF; the ISL is a Stage 3 addition.
  • Formation keeping in the propulsion budget. 60 m/s over three years for 150 km along-track, ±20 km cross-track, 5 km control — the same class of numbers HawkEye published.
  • Honest KPIs by stage. 5–20 km single-pass and 1–5 km multi-pass for TALOS-1; 0.3–3 km comms and 100–500 m radars for the cluster.
