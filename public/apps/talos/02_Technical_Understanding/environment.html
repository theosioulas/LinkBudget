02 Technical understanding · Environment

Environment, radiation & EMC

A 530 km, 53° orbit is benign for screened commercial electronics over three to five years, but it crosses the South Atlantic Anomaly on every orbit, cycles through eclipse seasons that swing the beta angle from near 0° to beyond 70°, and puts a −120 dBm receiver a metre from a 45 W transmitter. This page quantifies the radiation, thermal and electromagnetic environment the payload must survive, the debris and disposal rules that size the propulsion, and the launch loads that set the structure, with the mitigations the design carries for each. Thermal and decay figures are computed at page load; everything marked est. is an engineering estimate to be confirmed by analysis.

Radiation at 530 km

Total ionising dose at 500–600 km behind ≈ 2.5 mm of aluminium is in the 1–4 krad(Si) per year range depending on shielding and solar cycle est.; cubesat and microsat programmes use a mission design value of ≈ 20 krad(Si) for 500–600 km SSO (the CAMELOT platform specification is the public example)[R3-20]. A 53° orbit avoids the polar horns of the outer belt but crosses the South Atlantic Anomaly on every orbit, so its dose is comparable and dominated by trapped protons est.. The difference that matters is single-event effects: the SAA and the polar regions raise upset rates by an order of magnitude or more (count rates 60× higher near the poles in the CAMELOT data), and a commercial 16 nm RFSoC will see them. The design assumption is therefore not "no upsets" but "upsets several times a month, none of them fatal" (research 03 §7).

Mitigation for a COTS RFSoC payload

The approach is the one dozens of LEO missions have flown with Zynq-7000 and UltraScale+ devices: commercial parts, systematic mitigation, lot-level testing, and a radiation-tolerant supervisor that can always regain control. The space-grade XQR RFSoC remains an option if budget allows; the Teledyne EV12AQ600 + PolarFire RT architecture (150 / 100 krad) is the fallback if the COTS route fails its tests[R2-x79].

In the design

  • Configuration-memory scrubbing (AMD SEM IP) running continuously; readback CRC every few seconds.
  • Triple modular redundancy on control and state-machine logic; data-path logic unprotected but checked (frame CRCs, PDW sanity bounds).
  • ECC on DDR4 and on the 2 TB NAND with periodic scrubbing; file system journaling.
  • Latch-up current monitors with 50 µs trip and automatic power cycle on all COTS rails; supervisor logs every event.
  • Watchdogs at three levels (RFSoC application, supervisor, bus OBC) with staged resets: application restart → RFSoC reconfigure → payload power cycle.
  • Gold bitstream and software images in protected flash; uploaded images run from A/B slots with automatic rollback.
  • Local spot shielding (tantalum or 2–3 mm Al) on the RFSoC, NAND and tuner; the payload enclosure counts toward the 2.5 mm.
  • Acceptance that the payload resets several times a month: the collection schedule resumes from the supervisor's copy, and a dwell lost to a reset is logged as a gap, not an absence of emitters.

Parts screening approach

  • Standard: ECSS-Q-ST-60-13 (commercial EEE components) tailored as ESA does for IOD and Scout-class missions — screening and radiation lot testing instead of Class 1/2 parts (research 05 §2).
  • TID: Co-60 lot testing of the RFSoC, tuner transceiver, NAND, DDR, PCDU converters and the ADCs to 20–30 krad; MMIC LNAs/mixers accepted on heritage data.
  • SEE: proton testing (≈ 200 MeV) of the RFSoC, tuner silicon, NAND and the supervisor's memory; heavy-ion LET threshold for latch-up on the RFSoC and tuner rails — if below 40 MeV·cm²/mg, the current-trip design is mandatory, not optional.
  • Derating and construction analysis: per ECSS-Q-ST-30-11; destructive physical analysis on one device per critical lot.
  • Lot control: flight and test parts from the same date code; buy the flight lot early (long-lead list).
  • Lifetime position: 3-year warranted life with a 5-year goal, as research 05 recommends after Kleos' lifetime shortfall warning (T-18).

Thermal environment and eclipse seasons

At 53° inclination the orbit plane precesses relative to the Sun (J2 nodal regression ≈ °/day), so the beta angle — the angle between the Sun and the orbit plane — sweeps through its full range several times a year. Near β = 0° the satellite spends the maximum ≈ 36 % of each orbit in eclipse; above β ≈ 70° it is in continuous sunlight for a few days and the radiators see the Sun at low angles. Both extremes matter: eclipse sets the battery depth of discharge (36 min × ≈ 250 W = 150 Wh, 30 % of the 420 Wh pack in components.js), while the high-beta season sets the hot case for the RFSoC radiator and the cold-start case for the battery after long eclipses. Two-day samples through 2030; the SSO alternative holds a nearly constant beta angle and a constant ≈ 30 % eclipse, which is why thermal design is easier for Earth-observation missions than for a regional-persistence orbit. The 53° orbit's beta cycle period (≈ days) is also the period over which the solar-array power and the payload duty cycle must be planned.

Payload thermal design

  • RF front-ends for the four DF chains and the LO on a temperature-controlled plate at 20 ± 2 °C (PID heaters, thermistors read by the payload controller) so inter-chain phase stays within the 5° budget between calibrations (T-20).
  • RFSoC (48 W) and tuner (14 W) on a variable-conductance heat pipe to a 0.25 m² radiator on the −X face, which never sees nadir and faces away from the +X downlink antenna; junction ≤ 85 °C in the hot case (β > 60°, full collection, downlink pass).
  • Payload radiator decoupled from bus radiators so that bus heater cycles do not modulate the front-end temperature.
  • Collection duty cycle is a thermal lever: in the hot case the RFSoC may be held to 70 % duty over the sunlit arc, which costs almost nothing in collection because the SAA crossing is in the same part of the orbit for much of the beta cycle.
  • Deployed helices and the carbon-fibre cross-boom see ±150 °C swings; the boom's near-zero CTE and the calibration comb absorb what the structure does not.

UHF-quiet bus rules (Phase A requirement)

  • Radiated emissions from any bus unit in 225–512 MHz at 1 m: below −130 dBm/Hz equivalent at the UHF antenna port est. — specified to the bus vendor as a measured requirement, not a design goal.
  • All switching converters synchronised; frequencies and harmonics documented in an emissions register.
  • No unshielded high-speed links on the nadir side; harness twisted-shielded pairs with 360° backshell termination.
  • Single-point grounding scheme with separate payload and bus returns; chassis bonding ≤ 2.5 mΩ.
  • "Quiet mode" command that stops magnetorquers, holds wheel speeds and parks non-essential digital units for a dwell.
  • Transmit schedules (TT&C, X-band, ISL) published to the payload controller so receive blanking is deterministic.

EMC test plan

  • Unit level: conducted and radiated emissions of every bus unit and the payload PCDU (MIL-STD-461 / ECSS-E-ST-20-07 tailored), with the UHF band measured at the payload's own sensitivity rather than at the standard limit.
  • EM level (Phase C): bus engineering model plus payload EM in an anechoic chamber: receive noise floor in every band with all bus units cycling; transmit-to-receive isolation with the X-band and S-band transmitters keyed; blanking timing verified to the microsecond.
  • Flight model: repeat of the self-compatibility test after integration; record the interference fingerprint that flies as the first entry of the on-orbit mask table.
  • Facility: HAITF in Greece if the chamber is available, otherwise an EU facility (research 05 §AIT); budget €0.5–1.5 M for the whole environmental campaign.
  • Pathfinder: if the hosted-payload pathfinder flies, its first job is to measure a real bus's emissions against a real UHF receiver (research 05 §6.4 lists T-02 among the risks it retires).

Debris, decay and disposal

The FCC's 5-year post-mission disposal rule (adopted 29 September 2022)[R3-21][R5-30] and ESA's Zero Debris approach (prompt disposal, ≥ 90 % disposal success probability under ESSB-ST-U-007)[R3-22] set the standard TALOS will be held to, whichever licence it flies under; the draft EU Space Act codifies the same obligations for EU operators[R5-12] and insurers price Charter compliance[R5-19]. Natural decay from 530 km takes well over five years for a 150 kg satellite, so controlled lowering is mandatory. The chart is a simple ballistic estimate: an exponential atmosphere at three solar-activity levels and a ballistic coefficient computed from the TALOS mass and area — good enough to show the shape of the problem, not a substitute for a DRAMA or STELA run.

Budget check

Collision avoidance is the other half of T-19: the 500–600 km shell is the most congested in LEO, and a 150 kg satellite with 1 mN of thrust needs ≈ 1 day of warning to open a 1 km miss distance. The baseline therefore assumes a conjunction-assessment service subscription (KSAT/LeoLabs class) and keeps 10 m/s of the ΔV budget for avoidance over the mission, inside the 40 m/s drag make-up allocation.

Launch environment

TALOS-1 is sized for an ESPA-class port (15-inch ring, 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.8 m stowed envelope, ≤ 150 kg) on a rideshare mission — SpaceX Transporter/Bandwagon or a European small launcher once available (research 02 §L).
