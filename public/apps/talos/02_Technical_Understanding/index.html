02 Technical understanding · System architecture

System architecture

TALOS-1 is a 530 km / 53° ESPA-class satellite whose payload is a dual-path passive receiver: a high-dynamic-range comms tuner for UHF and L-band and a direct-sampling RFSoC for the S/C/X radar bands, fed by a survey-plus-interferometer antenna suite and tied to GNSS time at the 20 ns level. This page shows the whole system end-to-end, explains the one architectural decision that shapes everything else, and lists the design drivers and top-level requirements that the following chapters justify.

The dual-path receiver decision

A single receiver type cannot serve both halves of the mission. Comms signals (25 kHz voice, TETRA, Link-16 hops, GNSS jammers) are weak, narrow and long; they need high dynamic range in narrow bins and long integration. Radar pulses are strong, wide and short (0.08–1 µs for a magnetron marine radar); they need gigahertz of instantaneous bandwidth and nanosecond time-of-arrival, and the ADC must survive a −24 dBm mainlobe pulse from an AN/SPY-1 without clipping[R3-16]. Decision D-01 — dual-path receiver

TALOS-1 carries both. The comms tuner is the HawkEye Pathfinder lineage grown from 56 MHz to 200 MHz of instantaneous bandwidth; the RFSoC is the Xiphos Q8RF / ZU48DR class that HawkEye adopted for its later clusters[R2-x84]. The split mirrors how modern ESM suites separate "comms ESM" from "radar ESM" and, crucially, it is also a dynamic-range split: a saturating S-band pulse cannot desensitise the UHF channel because they never share an ADC (research 03 §2.6). The cost is two firmware stacks and ≈ 14 W + 48 W of digitiser power; the EU-sourced alternative for the radar path (Teledyne EV12AQ600 + PolarFire RT) is kept as the export-control fallback[R2-x79]. Design drivers

Six physical facts shape the payload more than any customer requirement does. Each is developed on its own page; the summary here is what a reviewer should hold in mind when reading the budgets.

Driver 1 · radars

Dynamic range, not sensitivity

At 2000 km an AN/SPY-1 mainlobe (≈ 140 dBm EIRP) arrives at −24 dBm while its −35 dB sidelobes still give 43 dB SNR in 10 MHz; a 25 kHz tactical radio at the same range sits near −105 dBm. The receiver must hold ≈ 95–100 dB of instantaneous range, which no single ADC does. Hence band segregation, limiters, microsecond step attenuators, ADC over-range blanking and headroom set so the thermal floor is only 10–15 dB above the LSB (research 03 §2.6). See emitters.

Driver 2 · comms

Frequency coverage and instantaneous bandwidth

Narrowband comms are comfortably above the floor with 5–10 dBi of gain; what limits them is being tuned to the right place at the right time. Link-16 hops over 255 MHz, a TETRA network uses 25 kHz slots, a PTT burst lasts seconds. The tuner's 200 MHz IBW with a 4096-bin polyphase filter bank is the answer: stare at a whole band, detect in matched 25 kHz bins (26 dB better than a 10 MHz bin), then snapshot.

Driver 3 · geometry

Field of regard versus gain

Seeing the whole footprint above 10° elevation from 530 km needs a ±66° cone about nadir, which caps a staring antenna at 4–8 dBi. A 27 dBi beam covers 1/100 of that solid angle. TALOS therefore pairs wide-FOV survey elements (alarm) with nadir-pointing S/X arrays and a 4-element interferometer (characterise and locate), and keeps a 0.7 m reflector as a cued "zoom" growth option. See antenna trade study.

Driver 4 · timing

Nanoseconds for TDOA, 10−11 for FDOA

1 ns of clock error is 30 cm of range difference; HawkEye budgets 20 ns between satellites[R3-2]. FDOA on a 1 GHz carrier needs the two LOs to agree to ≪ 0.1 Hz over a 100 ms snapshot, i.e. 10−10–10−11. A GNSS-disciplined OCXO with CSAC holdover and raw GNSS observables logged with every snapshot (common-view time transfer) meets this without an inter-satellite link. See geolocation theory.

Driver 5 · EMC

A quiet bus around a −120 dBm receiver

The satellite's own S-band TT&C, X-band downlink, DC/DC converters, wheel drivers and clocks are 100+ dB stronger than the signals of interest, and the X-band transmitter is only 200 MHz from the X-band receive window. Transmitters and receive arrays sit on opposite faces, the payload has its own filtered PCDU, receive is blanked during transmission, and a "UHF-quiet" rule is written into the bus specification. See environment & EMC.

Driver 6 · data

Reduce on board or drown

Four tuner channels at 100 MHz and 12 bits produce 9.6 Gbit/s of raw IQ (≈ 104 TB/day); the downlink moves ≈ 100–500 GB/day. Everything must become pulse descriptor words, emitter reports, signal reports and prioritised 20 kB–5 MB snapshots on orbit, which is why the payload carries a controller, a 2 TB store and (optionally) an ML co-processor. See on-board processing & data.
