02 Technical understanding · Antennas

Antenna trade study

No single antenna covers 225 MHz to 12 GHz, and no antenna that fits a 150 kg bus has both wide field of regard and high gain. The TALOS-1 suite therefore has three kinds of aperture: deployable helices and wideband survey elements that see the whole ±66° field of regard at 3–10 dBi, body-mounted S- and X-band patch arrays that add 18–22 dBi where radars live, and a four-element sinuous interferometer on a 1.2 m cross-boom for single-satellite direction finding. This page gives the physics, the candidate types with sketches, what operators actually fly, the interferometer design with a calculator, and the supplier shortlist.

Baseline: TALOS-1 v0.3Sources: research notes 02 §B, 03 §3

Fundamentals: gain, beamwidth, footprint

For an aperture antenna of effective diameter D at wavelength λ, with aperture efficiency η (0.5–0.7 for reflectors and arrays):

$$ G \approx \eta\left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; G_{dBi} \approx 20\log_{10}\frac{D}{\lambda} + 9.94 + 10\log_{10}\eta $$ $$ \theta_{3dB} \approx 70^\circ\,\frac{\lambda}{D}, \qquad \text{footprint diameter at range } d \approx d\,\theta_{3dB}\,[\text{rad}] $$

Gain and beamwidth are two faces of the same number: every 6 dB of gain halves the beamwidth and quarters the solid angle covered. For an axial-mode helix of N turns, circumference C ≈ λ and turn spacing S ≈ 0.25 λ, the Kraus formula gives \( G \approx 15\,N\,S\,C^2/\lambda^3 \) and \( \theta_{3dB} \approx 52^\circ/(C\sqrt{NS}) \) — the gain comes from length, not area, which is why it is the UHF answer.

Kraus' formula is optimistic by 1–3 dB for short helices; vendors quote 8–13 dBi for 5–12 turns (research 03 §3.2). Bandwidth is ≈ 1.5:1, so one helix cannot cover 225–512 MHz at full gain — the TALOS helices are centred for 250–450 MHz with the wideband CP antennas covering the edges at lower gain.

Field of regard versus gain

From 530 km, the ground visible above 10° elevation is a circle of radius km (≈ million km²); to see all of it the antenna must cover a cone of ±° about nadir, and ±° for the 5° horizon. This half-angle is the fundamental tension: a wide-FOR staring element is capped at roughly 4–8 dBi, whereas a 27 dBi beam at 3 GHz covers 1/100 of that solid angle. The drawing is to scale — the satellite is close to the Earth, which is why the useful cone is so wide and so flat.

Interferometric direction finding

A single satellite measures a line of bearing by comparing the phase of the same signal at two elements a baseline D apart. For an arrival angle θ from boresight:

$$ \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi D}{\lambda}\sin\theta, \qquad \sigma_\theta = \frac{\lambda\,\sigma_\phi}{2\pi D\cos\theta}\ [\text{rad}] $$ $$ \text{ground error} \approx d\,\sigma_\theta \ \text{(cross-range)}, \quad \text{worse by } 1/\sin\varepsilon \text{ along the line of sight} $$

Research 03 §3.4 gives the reference case: D = 1 m, 1 GHz (D/λ = 3.3), σφ = 5° → σθ = 4.2 mrad → 2.1 km at nadir from 500 km, 4–6 km at 1000 km. At 300 MHz the same baseline gives 13 mrad; at 3 GHz, 1.4 mrad. The phase budget is the hard part: 5° at 1 GHz is 4 mm of path — thermally stable cabling, matched LNAs and on-orbit calibration against known beacons (AIS base stations, ADS-B ground references, dedicated calibration transmitters). Attitude knowledge enters directly: 1 mrad of attitude error is 1 km at 1000 km, which is why TALOS carries star trackers (0.02° knowledge).

Ambiguity. Phase wraps when D > λ/2, so a 1.2 m baseline at 1 GHz (4 λ) has ≈ 8 ambiguous lobes across ±90°. The resolutions are a short unambiguous baseline plus long precise ones (coarse/fine), three or more non-collinear elements (planar correlative interferometry), and combining AOA with the Doppler history over the pass. Correlative interferometry with 4–6 wideband elements over 1–12 GHz is the standard ESM approach and tolerates mutual coupling via calibration tables.

Polarisation

Ground communications are mostly vertical, marine radars horizontal, aircraft antennas vary, and the view from orbit rotates the apparent polarisation as the satellite passes. Faraday rotation adds tens of degrees at 300 MHz (scaling ≈ 1/f², about 1° at 3 GHz). A single linear polarisation risks 10–30 dB fades; dual-polarised elements with diversity combining limit the loss to ≤ 3 dB and give polarisation ratio as a classification feature (research 03 §3.6). The TALOS rule is therefore: everything dual-polarised. The two UHF helices are wound in opposite senses (RHCP + LHCP), the wideband survey antennas are an RHCP/LHCP pair, the sinuous elements are dual-circular by construction, and the patch arrays are dual-linear (H/V) with both outputs digitised — which is why the S/C/X bank has eight chains for six antennas.

Swept beam versus wide field of view

The search-rate argument (research 03 §3.5): a staring 70° beam sees the whole footprint continuously at ≈ 7 dBi. A 7° beam (27 dBi at 3 GHz) covers 1/100 of the solid angle and must revisit 100 cells per pass; with ≈ 8 minutes above 10° that is ≈ 5 s per cell — longer than a radar scan period (fine for radars) but short compared with a push-to-talk burst (poor for comms). The compromise every ESM system uses, and TALOS follows, is wide-FOV omnidirectional channels for detection and alarm, plus one or two high-gain channels cued for characterisation and precise AOA. In TALOS-1 the high-gain channels are fixed nadir arrays; the 0.7 m reflector option makes one of them steerable (through the spacecraft, at 0.05° pointing), and a Vivaldi digital-beamforming panel is the Stage 3 way to steer without moving anything.

Decision D-03 — survey + arrays + interferometer, reflector deferred

TALOS-1 flies the full survey and DF suite and defers the deployable reflector. Reasons: (1) the products that pay in Stage 1 (radars, tactical comms, jammers) need coverage and dynamic range, not 30 dBi; (2) a deployable reflector is the long pole in the antenna category (12–18 months, £0.5–1.5 M, price unverified) and the main schedule risk[R2-x63]; (3) it forces 0.05° pointing onto the bus for every collection, whereas the baseline arrays tolerate the ADCS easily. The structural interface (+X face HDRM) and the RF chain inputs are reserved so that it can be added in Stage 2 without redesign.

Deployables, risk and calibration

Three deployments stand between launch and first light: the two UHF helices (single-shot HDRM and hinge each), the DF cross-boom (two booms, four HDRMs) and the solar wings. Deployment failure is risk T-01; the boom's thermal distortion and the chains' phase drift are risk T-20. The mitigation plan:

Mechanism qualification

  • Buy flight-proven helices and booms (Oxford Space Systems, Helical Communication Technologies, Kall Morris class) rather than develop; TRL 9 for helices, 6–8 for booms.
  • Zero-g offload deployment tests at ambient, hot and cold; vibration at ESPA levels before and after.
  • Redundant release (dual initiators or burn-wire plus pin-puller); deployment telemetry on every hinge; no deployment within the first orbit.
  • Graceful degradation: with one helix lost, the other still covers UHF at single polarisation; with one DF element lost, three remain and the 2-D LOB degrades to a 1-D cone plus Doppler.

Phase calibration plan

  • Ground: anechoic-chamber calibration of the full spacecraft (not the bare antenna) across 1–12 GHz and ±60°, stored as correlative-interferometry tables; the Hellenic AIT facility or an EU chamber.
  • Orbit, every orbit: the injected calibration comb measures each chain's gain and phase relative to the others, removing LNA and cable drift.
  • Orbit, every day: known emitters (AIS base stations, ADS-B ground stations, cellular sites of known position, dedicated beacons in Greece) fix the remaining structural and attitude biases.
  • Thermal: front-ends co-located on a ±2 °C plate, phase-stable coax matched to ±2 mm; the boom is carbon-fibre with near-zero CTE.
