On-board processing & data

The payload digitises about 10 Gbit/s on the comms path and 560 Gbit/s on the radar path; the X-band link can carry a few hundred gigabytes a day. Everything in between — channelisation, detection, pulse descriptor words, deinterleaving, signal reports, triggered IQ snapshots and on-board catalogue matching — exists to close that gap by three to four orders of magnitude while keeping what the ground needs for TDOA/FDOA and signature building. This page sizes each stage, computes the real pass statistics for Greek and polar ground stations for the TALOS orbit, and sets out the latency ladder and the software stack.

Why raw IQ downlink is infeasible

The raw data rate of N coherent channels of bandwidth B digitised at n bits per I and Q sample is

$$ R_{IQ} = 2\,B\,n_{bits}\,N_{ch} $$

Research 03 §5.1 gives the anchor cases: four channels at 100 MHz and 12 bits are 9.6 Gbit/s (≈ 104 TB/day); eight RFSoC channels at 5 GSPS and 14 bits are 560 Gbit/s raw. A good X-band downlink delivers 100–500 GB/day, i.e. 0.1–0.5 % of the modest four-channel case. Move the sliders to see how little of the problem a faster downlink solves.

The processing chain

The chain is the same one every ESM system implements, split along the two receiver paths (research 03 §5.2). Rates on the wires are design-level estimates for TALOS-1 in collection mode; the point of the figure is the ratio between adjacent stages, not the last digit.

1 · Channelisation and detection

A polyphase filter bank splits each path into bins matched to the signals: 4096 × 25 kHz across 100 MHz for comms, 64 × 31 MHz across 2 GHz for radar. An order-statistic CFAR estimates the noise floor per bin and declares detections 10–13 dB above it (Pfa ≈ 10−6). Matching the bin to the signal is worth up to 26 dB for a 25 kHz voice channel compared with a 10 MHz bin — more than any antenna upgrade.

2 · Radar path — PDWs and deinterleaving

Each threshold crossing becomes a pulse descriptor word: time of arrival (≈ 1 ns with interpolation), pulse width, amplitude, frequency, AOA phase from the coherent chains, polarisation ratio and an intra-pulse modulation flag. PDWs are clustered by AOA/frequency/PW, then PRI analysis (CDIF/SDIF histograms, PRI transform) separates interleaved trains and labels PRI type, scan period and agility pattern. Output ≈ 1–2 kB per emitter per dwell plus a short IQ exemplar of 10–100 pulses.

3 · Comms path — signal reports

For each occupied channel: centre frequency, bandwidth, start/stop, power, AOA, modulation class and symbol rate (cyclostationary features or a CNN on IQ / spectrogram), and hop-pattern statistics for frequency-hopping nets. Inference fits on the FPGA's DPU or on the optional ML co-processor (Ubotica / Jetson class, 1–40 W).

4 · Snapshots and catalogue matching

On a signal-of-interest match, an area-of-interest hit or novelty, the controller stores a time-stamped IQ snapshot for ground TDOA/FDOA cross-correlation and fine modulation-on-pulse analysis. Reports are compared on board with the uploaded library (frequency, PRI, PW, scan, MOP; modulation and bandwidth for comms) to rank the downlink queue and, in the cluster phase, to raise alerts over the ISL within minutes.

Snapshots, storage and the data budget

Snapshot sizes follow from decimated rate × duration × 2 × 16 bits (research 03 §5.3). The default rates — 2000 comms and 500 radar snapshots per orbit — give ≈ 1–3 GB per orbit and 15–45 GB per day, which is the figure used for the mass-memory sizing in components.js (2 TB buffers one to two weeks). Downlink passes: Greek site versus polar

The numbers below are not quoted from the research — they are computed when the page loads by propagating the TALOS-1 orbit (530 km, 53°) and its SSO alternative for one day against the ground stations in baseline.js, counting passes above the baseline minimum elevation of ° (and 10° for comparison). The last column is the downlink volume at 1 Gbit/s with 70 % link efficiency over the 5° contact time. Research 04 anchors them: Svalbard sees essentially every SSO orbit, a mid-latitude site such as Nemea 4–6 contacts a day[R4-33]. Counts are two-day averages (they vary by ±1–2 from day to day as the ground track drifts) and the longest gap is taken over the 48 h run, so read the table as typical, not guaranteed. Note the behaviour of a 53° orbit seen from 38° latitude: passes come on several consecutive orbits (≈ 95 min apart) and then stop for half a day — the longest-gap column, not the pass count, is what sets latency.

What the pass arithmetic says

Transmitter classes to match: ≈ 100 Mbit/s X-band units (Syrlinks EWC27, ~0.3 kg, ~10 W DC)[R3-26] up to the ≈ 1 Gbit/s class (Syrlinks XONOS, IQ Spacecom XLink-X; DVB-S2 / CCSDS 131.2) at 20–40 W DC[R3-27]. TALOS-1 carries the 1 Gbit/s class with a 15 dBi antenna, costing 45 W during passes (components.js). HawkEye Pathfinder managed on 3–50 Mbit/s[R3-1] because its products were already reduced on board — the lesson being that the downlink is a convenience, not the enabler.

Latency ladder

Latency is the time from an emission to a report on a user's screen. Store-and-forward with a single mid-latitude station is the Stage 1 reality; the ladder shows what each addition buys. HawkEye's own filings describe moving "from hours to minutes" with direct downlink, crosslinks and on-board processing[R4-2]; Unseenlabs cut delivery time by 3× through KSAT network integration[R3-6].

    Decision D-05 — ground first, orbit later

    Stage 1 runs the full classifier and all geolocation on the ground from downlinked snapshots and reports; the on-board chain is limited to channelisation, detection, PDW extraction, deinterleaving and library matching for prioritisation. This mirrors the Kleos ground-correlation reference architecture and keeps the flight software small enough to qualify[R3-10]. On-board classification and cross-ambiguity move up when the cluster and the ISL make minute-latency alerts a sellable product (Stage 2–3), with the A/B application slots designed in from the start so the migration is an upload, not a redesign.
