Target emitters & detectability

Eighteen emitter classes from tactical radios to naval radars, and whether TALOS-1 can see them from 530 km. The short answer: radars and most military communications yes, strong jammers yes, 10 mW GNSS jammers and consumer drone links no. Radars are never limited by sensitivity but by dynamic range; communications are limited by being tuned to the right band at the right time. Every number on this page is computed with the same link-budget functions the rest of the study uses.

Mainlobe versus sidelobe, pulses and duty cycle

Two facts about radars decide the receiver design. First, a radar's sidelobes are only 25–45 dB below its mainlobe, and from LEO even the sidelobes of a surveillance radar are tens of dB above the noise floor: an AN/SPY-1 at 2000 km gives ≈ 43 dB SNR in 10 MHz through −35 dB sidelobes and still 18 dB through −60 dB far sidelobes (case g). TALOS therefore treats sidelobe detection as the baseline and mainlobe hits as high-SNR bonus events for fine modulation-on-pulse analysis. Published sidelobe ranges: parabolic reflectors −20 to −30 dB (first) and −40 to −50 dB (far); planar phased arrays −30 to −45 dB; marine slotted waveguides −25 to −30 dB; backlobes −40 to −60 dB (research 03 §2.5).

Second, duty cycle decides the detector, not the budget. A 1 µs pulse at 1 kHz PRF is 0.1 % duty: integrated over a 1 ms FFT bin its energy is 30 dB below the peak, so a long-FFT comms detector would miss a radar that a microsecond-scale detector sees at full peak power. Radar detection therefore uses short-time energy detection on wideband channels, comms detection uses long FFTs on narrow channels — the dual-path architecture in the RF chain page follows directly. The hardest radar class is the low-peak-power FMCW / solid-state type (coastal and modern marine radars at 1–10 W average spread over 10–50 MHz), which needs bandwidth-matched de-chirp or long integration.

Dynamic range and ADC saturation

The payload must resolve a −120 dBm comms signal while a −24 dBm radar mainlobe pulse arrives in the same field of regard: roughly 95–100 dB of instantaneous dynamic range, more than any single ADC chain delivers (research 03 §2.6). The −24 dBm number comes from case (g): a 6 MW peak AN/SPY-1[R3-16] with an estimated 42 dBi antenna gives ≈ 140 dBm EIRP; after 168.8 dB of path loss at 2000 km and 5 dBi of receive gain the mainlobe arrives at dBm — about the level of a Wi-Fi router one metre away. A 14-bit converter clipped by such a pulse generates broadband intermodulation that destroys the whole 2 GHz Nyquist band for the duration of the pulse. The mitigations, in the order they appear in the chain:

1 · Band segregation

Separate RF chains and ADCs for UHF/L (comms-dominated) and S/C/X (radar-dominated), with > 60 dB band filters between them, so a saturating S-band pulse cannot desensitise the UHF channel. This is the dual-path architecture.

2 · Limiters and fast attenuators

PIN limiters ahead of the LNA protect the first stage; 0–31 dB step attenuators with microsecond response implement a fast AGC. The chain must recover within a few µs of a strong pulse to preserve the time of arrival of the next one.

3 · ADC headroom and processing gain

A 14-bit RF ADC with ENOB ≈ 9–10 gives 56–62 dB over Nyquist; in a 1 MHz bin at 5 GSPS the processing gain 10·log(fs/2B) adds ≈ 34 dB, so ≈ 90–95 dB of spur-free range per bin — only if the converter is not clipped. Gain is set so the thermal floor sits 10–15 dB above the LSB, leaving 60–70 dB to full scale.

4 · Pulse blanking and the high-ENOB comms path

The ADC over-range flag blanks and tags the affected samples in the comms channels. The comms path itself uses 16-bit, 250 MSPS converters (ENOB 12–13) behind a tuner, giving a cleaner 80 dB range than direct RF sampling at 5 GSPS.

What this means for the budgets

Sensitivity is cheap: a 2.5 dB noise figure and 5 dBi already see most military communications. Dynamic range is expensive: it costs a second receiver path, two sets of filters, limiters and attenuators in every chain, and most of the digitiser power. When someone proposes a lower-cost single-receiver payload, the question to ask is what happens to the UHF channel when an AEW&C radar flies through the footprint.

Key takeaways for sales

These are the claims the engineering supports, written so that a proposal can quote them without qualification, and the claims it does not. The research is explicit that "drone detection from LEO" must be read as detection of ground control stations and military-class links, not of consumer aircraft (research 03 §1, takeaway iii).

can claim Detect and characterise

  • Surveillance, air-defence, AEW&C, naval, coastal and marine navigation radars — through their sidelobes, from anywhere in the footprint, including EMCON changes and new radar sites.
  • Tactical VHF/UHF push-to-talk, TETRA base stations, UHF SATCOM uplinks and L-band satphone bursts (SNR 15–40 dB in 25 kHz).
  • Link-16 / IFF network activity and hop statistics (per-pulse SNR ≈ 8–13 dB; robust with integration).
  • Military-class GNSS jammers (≥ 100 W) and all EW / communications jammers; the spectral shape classifies them.
  • Military UAS ground control stations (10–20 W directional links) and the pattern of life around them.

cannot claim Below the floor or needs cueing

  • Consumer UAS links (DJI-class, 26–33 dBm EIRP): SNR −9 to −16 dB even at nadir; +10 dB would need a ≈ 30 dBi, 1.5 m dish.
  • 10 mW GNSS "personal privacy devices": −46 dB SNR, −23 dB even after de-chirp; detectable only statistically as an elevated noise floor over dense areas.
  • C/X-band VSAT uplinks through sidelobes: ≈ 0 dB SNR with 15 dBi; marginal without the 0.7 m reflector option or external cueing.
  • Message content: the architecture extracts externals (frequency, time, bandwidth, modulation, power, location); demodulation is a separately governed mode, as for every commercial operator[R3-30].
  • Transmitter power: a detection is an EIRP lower bound, not a measurement, unless geolocation and antenna-pattern knowledge are combined.

Operational precedents to cite with each claim: HawkEye 360 geolocates VHF/UHF push-to-talk radios, L-band satellite devices and marine radars as standing products[R3-3]; Aireon and Spire receive 1090 MHz ADS-B from LEO with 8–10 dBi antennas; Unseenlabs and Kleos built their maritime products on X-band navigation radars[R3-6][R3-9]; the GNSS-jammer power classes come from an 18-jammer measurement study[R3-15].
