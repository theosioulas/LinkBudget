04 Service demo · Ground segment

Ground segment assumptions

TALOS does not need to own a ground network. It needs one owned S-band TT&C and tasking station on Greek soil, X-band data reception bought per pass from KSAT's Nemea site a few kilometres away, two complementary GSaaS sites for latency, an EU sovereign cloud for the data platform and a small classified enclave for government products. This page sets out what other operators do, computes the contact statistics for the baseline orbit, sizes the data volume and compares service against ownership costs.

Decisions carried into the baseline

1. Buy X-band passes (GSaaS) from KSAT Nemea for the demonstrator; add X-band to the owned Greek station in the cluster phase, when the pass count makes it pay and sovereignty makes it necessary. 2. Own the S-band TT&C and tasking uplink near Athens so that command authority and crypto keys never leave Greek control. 3. Book KSAT Dubai and Puertollano as latency complements for the 53° orbit; Svalbard and Troll only if the first launch is sun-synchronous. 4. Host the data platform in an EU sovereign cloud with an on-premises classified enclave, designed to NIS2 from day one[R5-12].

What other operators do

Space-RF operators treat the ground network as a commodity bought per antenna-minute and keep their differentiation in processing. KSAT's KSATlite network — twenty-plus sites including Svalbard at 78°N, Troll in Antarctica and Nemea in the Peloponnese — covers S-band up/down, X-band down at 8.0–8.4 GHz and Ka-band, with per-pass pricing and a dedicated-antenna option[R4-33]. AWS Ground Station bills per antenna-minute, on demand or reserved with a twelve-month commitment, in narrowband (<40 MHz) and wideband (≥40 MHz) classes, with data landing directly in S3[R4-32][R2-x101]. Leaf Space runs a European S/X network of fifteen-plus sites with up to 1 Gbit/s X-band on its larger dishes[R2-x103]. Unseenlabs cut its delivery time threefold by integrating KSAT's network rather than building its own[R3-6].

TALOS ground architecture

The architecture separates three security zones. The public / provider zone holds the GSaaS antennas and the encrypted bulk transport from them; nothing in that zone can read the data. The corporate zone holds the owned TT&C station, mission control, the data platform and the unclassified product APIs. The classified zone is an air-gapped enclave fed by a one-way diode, where ELNOT mappings, national platform associations and government-only products live. Telecommand authority and the HSM-protected keys stay in the corporate zone in Greece; GSaaS providers only ever see AES-256 ciphertext.

Resulting latency tiers

Latency from collection to product is the sum of the wait for the next downlink contact, the pass itself and processing. The contact statistics above give the first term directly: with Nemea alone the worst-case wait is the site's maximum gap; adding Dubai and Puertollano shortens it because the three sites' windows interleave around the 53° ground track; a polar site makes it one orbit for an SSO. Processing adds about 25 min from loss of signal to a Level 2 product in the demo console's assumption, and HawkEye's own target is to move from "hours" to "minutes" with direct downlink, crosslinks and on-board processing[R4-2].

Data volume per pass

At the baseline 1 Gbit/s X-band rate[R3-27] a single pass moves tens of gigabytes — far more than the 30 GB/day of reduced products (reports plus selected IQ snapshots) that the payload is sized to produce, and far less than the raw IQ of even the four-channel comms path, which is why reduction happens on board (see on-board processing & data). GSaaS cost versus an owned station

The research prices GSaaS at €40–250 per pass (est.) with KSAT's X-band market range at $150–500 per pass and AWS's historical $3–10 per antenna-minute[R5-20][R2-x100][R2-x101]; an owned dual-band X/S station in Greece (3.7–5 m) costs €1.0–2.5 M capex plus about €0.2 M/yr to run (est.)[R5-20], with 3.7 m X-band systems quoted at €300–800 k and 5 m Safran Cortex-class stations at €0.5–2 M (prices unverified). The chart draws annual cost against the number of X-band passes bought per day. At the chosen price the owned station breaks even at roughly — passes a day: the single demonstrator stays below that line, so it buys passes; a 3-satellite cluster downlinking separately crosses it at mid-range prices. That is why the baseline buys GSaaS for TALOS-1 and plans the X-band upgrade of the owned Athens-area station for the cluster phase — justified by sovereignty, with the cost argument as support rather than as the driver. An owned site still needs GSaaS complements for latency because it only sees the passes visible from one place.

Mission control functions

The build-versus-buy analysis classes mission control software as a commodity to be licensed and customised[R4-8], at €0.2–0.6 M/yr for licences and cloud or €1–3 M to build[R5-20]. What TALOS adds on top of a commercial MCS is specific to a formation-flying RF mission:

Flight dynamics and formation keeping

Orbit determination from GNSS state vectors in telemetry (metre-class), TLE cross-checks and conjunction screening via EU SST; propagation with J2 and drag; manoeuvre planning for the 150 km along-track / ±20 km cross-track cluster with a ±5 km control box (BASELINE.constellation.cluster). The FEEP budget reserves 60 m/s for formation acquisition and maintenance, 40 m/s for drag make-up and 100 m/s for low-thrust disposal, so every manoeuvre is accounted against a ≈235 m/s total (26 kN·s at ~110 kg).

Pass scheduling and contact management

Automated scheduling of TT&C (owned) and X-band (GSaaS) contacts against the collection deck: a downlink pass over Nemea blanks the X-band receiver, so the scheduler must weigh collection against downlink for every Mediterranean pass. Bookings go to KSAT through their API; the schedule is re-planned when a provider cancels (risk T-16).

Telemetry monitoring and anomaly detection

Limit checking, trend analysis and model-based anomaly detection on bus and payload telemetry: RFSoC temperature and SEU counters, LNA currents, OCXO/CSAC lock status, storage fill, battery depth of discharge against the 30 % limit. Rules escalate to the on-call engineer; the automated layer is what keeps staffing sub-linear from 1 to 12 satellites.

Tasking executor and key management

Collection decks are compiled on the ground, signed and uplinked over the owned S-band link; the executor on board applies them per orbit. Telecommand and telemetry are AES-256 encrypted end to end with keys in an HSM under Greek control; a key-rotation and emergency-command procedure is part of the security accreditation.

Security and accreditation

A SIGINT constellation is a target. The risk register scores a cyber-attack on the ground segment at 15 (likelihood 3 × severity 5) with mitigations that define the architecture: zero-trust network design, AES-256 on TC and TM, HSM key management, NIS2 compliance, regular red-team exercises and a segregated classified enclave (research 05, risk T-17). The draft EU Space Act proposes either a bespoke resilience chapter or full NIS2 applicability for operators, and while a 2030 demonstrator is likely to be grandfathered, the cluster and constellation will fall under it[R5-12]. Government products attract NATO and EU classification, which requires a Facility Security Clearance from the Greek National Security Authority, cleared personnel and an air-gapped SECRET processing enclave, run in parallel with an unclassified commercial line (research 05 §3.7). The EUSPA RFI's questions about classified handling and NSA clearances show that EU institutional buyers will audit this before contracting[R4-16].
